Protecting people behind the data

In the last lesson, you learned how bias and privacy problems get into AI systems through the data they're trained on. Now you'll learn about practical strategies for protecting people's data — from principles that guide what data should be collected, to techniques that hide identities, to a growing movement toward running AI models locally where your data never leaves your device.

These aren't just technical details. Every one of these strategies represents a choice about whose privacy matters and how much control individuals should have over their own information. Understanding them helps you evaluate whether an AI tool respects the people behind its data — including you.

Data Minimization — Collect Less, Risk Less

The simplest privacy principle, and the one most often ignored.

Open this section: why collecting less protects more

Data minimization is one of the simplest and most powerful privacy principles: collect only the data you actually need to accomplish your specific purpose, and nothing more.

Think about it this way: if a restaurant needs your phone number to call ahead a reservation, it doesn't also need your home address, birthdate, and social media accounts. But many apps operate on the opposite principle — collect everything available now and figure out what to do with it later. That's how data breaches happen, and how personal information ends up in AI training datasets without anyone's knowledge or consent.

Why data minimization matters for AI

  • Less exposure risk: The less personal data an AI system holds, the less damage a breach can cause.
  • Narrower bias surface: Collecting only relevant data reduces the chance of accidentally including biased proxy variables (like zip codes that correlate with race).
  • Clearer accountability: When a company collects only what it needs for a stated purpose, it's easier to audit whether it's using that data responsibly.

Data minimization in practice

  • An AI-powered job screening tool should collect resume text and job-relevant qualifications — not the applicant's age, gender, or photos (which could introduce bias and violate privacy).
  • A health-chatbot should collect symptoms and medical history relevant to its advice — not your location history, contacts list, or browsing habits.
  • An educational AI tutor should track learning progress — not record voice samples, monitor keystroke timing, or profile behavioral patterns beyond what's needed for the tutoring function.
Note

Key principle: Data minimization shifts the default assumption from "collect everything" to "collect only what's necessary." It's a design choice that protects privacy before problems happen, rather than trying to fix them after.

Anonymization Techniques — Hiding Identity Without Losing Utility

Four techniques, from the baseline to the most advanced — and why none of them is a finish line.

Open this section: four anonymization techniques and their limits

When data must be collected, anonymization is the process of removing or transforming identifying information so individuals can't be recognized. But as you learned in M1L4, true anonymization is harder than it sounds. Here's an overview of the main techniques and their limitations:

1. Direct Identifier Removal — the baseline

The simplest approach: strip names, email addresses, phone numbers, social security numbers, and other direct identifiers from a dataset. This is the baseline — but it's not enough on its own. A dataset of medical records without names can still be traced back to individuals when combined with other publicly available information.

2. Aggregation

Instead of storing individual data points, combine them into group-level statistics. For example, instead of recording "Person A visited these 15 websites," store "37% of users in this age group visited category X." Aggregation protects individuals well — but it also loses detail, which can reduce the usefulness of the data for training AI models.

3. Differential Privacy

A more advanced technique that adds carefully calibrated statistical "noise" to a dataset, making it mathematically difficult to determine whether any specific individual is in the data. Major organizations (including Apple and the U.S. Census Bureau) use differential privacy. It's one of the strongest anonymization methods available — but adding too much noise degrades data quality, and adding too little leaves individuals exposed. Finding the right balance requires expertise.

4. Data Synthesis

Instead of using real personal data, generate artificial (synthetic) datasets that preserve the statistical patterns of the original data without containing any actual individual records. Synthetic data is promising for AI training — but if the synthesis process isn't done carefully, it can reintroduce identifiable patterns or amplify biases present in the source data.

Caution

The hard truth: No anonymization technique is perfect. Researchers have repeatedly re-identified individuals from "anonymized" datasets by cross-referencing with public records, social media, or other databases. The best approach combines multiple techniques and treats anonymization as an ongoing process — not a one-time fix applied before the data is released.

Whose Account Are You Using?

The single most useful privacy fact in this course — and it's about you, this week.

Open this section: why the account, not the tool, sets the rules

Everything so far has been about what companies do with data they collect. Now here's the part that's about you, this week, in this course.

The account you sign in with — not the tool you pick — is what decides how your words are handled. The same product, opened in the same browser, on the same laptop, can operate under two completely different sets of rules depending on which email address you logged in with. This surprises almost everyone, and it's the single most useful privacy fact in this course.

Roughly, there are two tiers:

An account your college provides

You sign in with your @student.mendocino.edu address. The college has an agreement with the vendor, and that agreement usually adds protections a personal account doesn't get — most commonly a commitment that what you type isn't used to train the company's models.

An account you create yourself

You sign up with a personal email. There's no agreement between that company and anyone acting on your behalf. You get whatever the vendor's public terms say, which the vendor can change, and which often default to using your conversations to improve the product unless you go turn that off.

Two questions that sound like one

Here's where people get it wrong, and it's worth slowing down for:

Note

"Is it used for training?" and "Can anyone see it?" are different questions with different answers.

A college-provided tool can be covered by a firm no-training commitment and be logged in the college's systems, where an administrator with a documented reason can retrieve what you typed. Both things are true at once. "Not used for training" was never a promise that nobody can read it.

And the reverse trips people up even harder. A personal account you made yourself has fewer people able to look at your conversations — no college administrator, no institutional audit trail. That sounds like more privacy. What it actually means is there's no accountable party and nowhere to appeal. Nobody to ask what's retained, nobody obliged to answer, nobody who has to tell you if something changes. The oversight and the protection tend to come from the same place.

Neither tier is "the safe one." They're different arrangements with different trade-offs, and the literacy skill is being able to say which one you're in right now.

What this means for your work

  • For anything connected to this course, use your college account. It's free, it's already yours, and it's the tier with an agreement behind it.
  • Never type another person's information into any AI tool — a classmate's work, a name, anything from a job or a volunteer role. Your consent isn't theirs to give.
  • Assume "delete" doesn't mean "gone" until you've checked what a given tool actually does. Deleting from your view and deleting from the company's systems are different operations, and the second one often has a timer on it.
Verify

Don't take this lesson's word for any of it. Open the tool you actually use, find the account indicator (usually your avatar, top right), and confirm which address you're signed in as. Then find its privacy or data-controls setting and read what it says about training. Every tool guide in this course — Gemini, Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude — walks you to that exact screen, and How the tools handle your data puts the four tools side by side with sources and dates you can click. If what you find there disagrees with what you read here, the product screen wins — and tell me, because it means the guide needs updating.

A Privacy-First Alternative — Coming in Module 2

A pointer, not a lesson. Module 2 does this properly.

Open this section: a short preview of where your data goes

There's a growing alternative to cloud-based AI that addresses privacy from the opposite direction: what if your data never left your device in the first place? Some AI tools can run on your own computer instead of sending everything you type to a company's servers.

We won't define or compare this category of tool here — Module 2 introduces the vocabulary properly and walks you through exactly how it works, how it compares to the cloud tools you've been using, and when each makes sense. For now, the key takeaway is just that this option exists, and that it's part of a bigger tradeoff between privacy and convenience that runs throughout this course.

Outline a Privacy Policy

About 15 minutes here Activity M1L5-A1 · ungraded

In this activity, you'll use an AI tool to help outline a privacy policy for a hypothetical AI app. You're not writing a full legal document — just 3–5 bullet-point commitments that show you understand data protection principles. You'll complete and refine this outline during the opening 20 minutes of your week-3 studio session.

Step-by-step instructions:

  1. Open Gemini in a browser tab (or another tool if you prefer).
  2. Copy and paste this prompt into it: 
    I'm designing a hypothetical AI-powered study assistant app for college students. The app helps students summarize lecture notes, generate practice questions, and get explanations of difficult concepts. Help me outline a privacy policy with 3-5 key commitments about how the app will protect student data. Think about: what data should the app collect (and what it shouldn't), how student data is stored and protected, whether data is used to train models, and how students can control or delete their data. Frame each commitment as a clear promise to the user.
  3. Read the response and identify 3–5 commitments that cover different aspects of data protection (data minimization, storage/security, user control, model training transparency, etc.).
  4. Record your 3–5 bullet commitments in a document or the notes area below. For each bullet, add a brief note (1 sentence) explaining which data protection principle it addresses (minimization, anonymization, user consent, local processing, etc.).
  5. This outline will be completed during the week-3 studio session (opening 20 minutes). Bring your notes and be ready to refine them with peer feedback and instructor guidance.
