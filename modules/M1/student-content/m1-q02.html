Overview

Your first portfolio artifact — 100 points, due Sunday, September 6.

In this module, you learned how AI models are trained on datasets — and how the data going in shapes what the model can (and cannot) do well. In this assignment, you'll put those ideas to work by analyzing a real dataset for bias and representation gaps, then proposing practical strategies to fix them.

This is your first portfolio artifact. It demonstrates that you can evaluate AI-generated content and training data for accuracy, bias, and appropriateness using established criteria (LO-03).

Note

This assignment draws directly on two lessons: M1L4, where bias enters a dataset, and M1L5, on data protection. Both are worth having open while you work.

What You'll Do

You will analyze a dataset — either one provided below or one you find on your own — and produce a written analysis covering four parts:

Part 1 — Describe the dataset

In 1–2 paragraphs, explain what the dataset contains, where it comes from, and what kind of AI task it could be used for (classification, prediction, recommendation, etc.). Be accurate about its origin: if you're using Option A, say that it's a constructed teaching dataset with fictional outlets, not collected news. If you use an AI tool to help summarize or explore it, note that in your work.

Part 2 — Detect bias

Examine at least three samples or categories from the dataset and identify specific biases or representation gaps. For each one, explain:

  • What group, perspective, or category is underrepresented, misrepresented, or missing?
  • What evidence from the data supports this? (Quote or describe actual entries.)
  • What real-world harm could result if an AI model trained on this data were deployed?

Part 3 — Propose mitigation strategies

For each bias you identified, suggest at least one practical strategy to reduce it. Strategies might include collecting more representative data, reweighting samples, adding missing categories, or changing how the data was labeled. Explain why your strategy would work.

Part 4 — Reflect on ethical implications

In a final paragraph (at least 150 words), reflect on what this exercise teaches you about trusting AI systems. Consider: If you didn't know the dataset was biased, would you have caught it when using the AI tool? What responsibility do developers and users share when bias is found?

Format & Length

  • Written analysis, approximately 500–800 words total across all four parts.
  • Clear headings for each part.
  • When you reference specific data entries, quote or describe them directly.

Dataset Options

Choose one.

Option A — Provided dataset

Use the 50-headline news dataset embedded in this page, tagged by topic and source. Browse it with the interactive table below.

It's a teaching dataset, not a scrape — the eight outlets are fictional and the headlines were written for this assignment. The patterns in it are real patterns, deliberately built in; your job is to find and name them, and to say in Part 1 that this is a constructed dataset rather than a live feed.

Option B — Your own dataset

Find a small, public dataset in an area you care about (sports stats, movie reviews, product ratings, census tables, etc.) and explore it with an AI tool. Include a link to your source in your submission.

A few hundred rows is plenty — big enough to show a pattern, small enough that you can actually look at it.

Caution

What not to put into an AI tool. If you pick your own dataset, use public data only. Don't paste in anything containing real people's personal information, anything from a job, internship, or volunteer role, or anything a classmate made. That's the data-minimization principle from M1L5 applied to your own work: the fastest way to create a privacy problem is to hand someone else's information to a tool on their behalf. When in doubt, pick a different dataset — or use Option A.

Tools

You may use Gemini (your college Google account) plus up to two other free AI tools of your choice to explore the dataset, generate summaries, or brainstorm mitigation ideas. Gemini is the baseline everyone has; the other two are your call, and you can skip them entirely. If you want a guide: Gemini and Copilot are free with your college login; ChatGPT and Claude (18+) need a personal account. Before you sign in to any of them, How the tools handle your data sets out what each one does with what you type.

Whatever you use, note it. Include brief notes on which prompts you ran and what came back. That shows your process — it isn't a confession, and it isn't a shortcut. Part of what's being graded is whether you checked the tool's claims against the actual data rather than repeating them.

How It's Graded

100 points, reviewed by your instructor against the rubric below. Nothing on this page is scored automatically.

M1-Q02 rubric — 100 points total
Dimension Excellent (full points) Adequate (partial points) Needs Work (minimal points) Points
Depth of bias detection in dataset samples Meets the three-gap minimum or exceeds it, and every gap is supported by specific evidence from actual entries (counts, quoted headlines, named sources) and tied to a concrete AI-task consequence. Three gaps are present, but support is uneven — some carry real evidence, others are asserted, or the AI-impact link is vague. Fewer than three gaps, or gaps are generic ("data can be biased") without dataset-specific evidence. 30
Clarity of representation gap explanations Each gap is explained clearly: which group/category is affected, how it shows up in the data, and why it matters. Language is precise and accessible. Gaps are identified but explanations are uneven — some clear, some unclear or missing the "why it matters" connection. Representation gaps are listed without explanation, or the student confuses bias with general data quality issues. 25
Practicality of suggested mitigation strategies Strategies are specific, actionable, and matched to each identified bias; shows understanding of how data collection or labeling could be improved. Strategies are present but some are vague ("collect more data") without explaining how or what kind. Fewer than one strategy per gap, or strategies are unrealistic or unrelated to the biases identified. 25
Reflection on ethical implications Thoughtful reflection (150+ words) that connects personal findings to broader questions about AI trust, developer/user responsibility, and real-world consequences. Shows genuine engagement. Reflection meets length requirement but stays at a surface level — restates assignment findings without deeper insight. Reflection is under 150 words, missing, or does not address ethical implications meaningfully. 20
