What is AI, really?

You've probably heard the term "AI" everywhere — in news headlines, app stores, product ads, and even casual conversation. But what does it actually mean? In this lesson, we'll build a clear foundation of AI vocabulary so you can talk about these tools with confidence. No coding required — just concepts you can use right away.

Three Key Terms: AI, ML, and DL

Three words people use interchangeably that don't mean the same thing.

Think of these three terms as nested circles — each one inside the next:

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the broadest term. It refers to the field of computing systems that perform tasks typically requiring human intelligence, including perception, reasoning, learning, and decision-making. In this course, AI refers to deployed systems and tools, not theoretical concepts. When someone says "AI," they could be talking about anything from a spam filter to a self-driving car.

Machine Learning (ML) sits inside AI. It's a subset of AI in which systems learn patterns from data rather than following explicitly programmed rules. Instead of a programmer writing every rule ("if the email contains 'winner,' mark as spam"), the system studies thousands of examples and figures out the patterns on its own. ML covers supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning at a conceptual level — we'll dig into those types in the next lesson.

Deep Learning sits inside machine learning. It's a subset of ML using multi-layered neural networks to learn hierarchical representations from large datasets. You can think of it as machine learning on steroids — it needs more data and more computing power, but it can solve problems that simpler ML methods can't, like recognizing objects in photos or translating between languages in real time. We'll discuss deep learning conceptually throughout this course; no mathematical or architectural detail is required.

Note

Quick analogy: All squares are rectangles, but not all rectangles are squares. Similarly, all deep learning is machine learning, and all machine learning is AI — but not the other way around.

A Brief History of AI — How Did We Get Here?

Understanding where AI came from helps you understand what it can and can't do today.

  1. 1950s

    The Birth of the Idea

    • In 1950, mathematician Alan Turing asked the question: "Can machines think?" His famous "Turing Test" proposed that if a human couldn't tell whether they were conversing with a machine or another human, the machine could be said to exhibit intelligent behavior.
    • The term "Artificial Intelligence" was coined in 1956 at a landmark conference at Dartmouth College, where researchers first gathered to explore the idea seriously. Early optimism was enormous — some predicted AI would solve most intellectual tasks within a generation.
  2. 1960s–1970s

    First Hype and First Disappointment First AI Winter

    • Early programs could solve simple math problems, play checkers, and translate basic sentences between languages. But the limits quickly became clear: computers didn't have enough processing power or data to handle real-world complexity.
    • Funding dried up as promises went unmet. This period became known as the first "AI Winter" — a time when interest and investment in AI dropped sharply.
  3. 1980s–1990s

    Expert Systems and the Rise of Data Second AI Winter

    • "Expert systems" tried to capture human expertise in rule-based programs. They worked well in narrow domains (like diagnosing certain diseases) but were brittle — they broke down when faced with situations outside their programmed rules.
    • Machine learning began gaining ground as researchers realized that teaching computers from data was more flexible than programming every rule by hand.
    • A second AI Winter followed in the early 1990s when expert systems proved too expensive and limited to scale.
  4. 2000s

    The Data Explosion

    • The internet created massive amounts of data — exactly what machine learning models needed to thrive.
    • Companies like Google started using ML at scale: for search results, email filtering, and ad targeting. AI was working quietly behind the scenes, even if most people didn't realize it.
  5. 2010s

    Deep Learning Takes Off

    • Better hardware (especially GPUs, originally designed for video games) made it possible to train much larger models.
    • Breakthroughs happened fast: computers started beating humans at games like Go and chess, recognizing images with superhuman accuracy, and translating languages fluently.
    • By the late 2010s, early large language models (like GPT-2 and GPT-3) showed that AI could generate coherent, human-like text — a capability that would explode into public awareness in the next decade.
  6. 2020s

    Generative AI Goes Mainstream

    • Tools like ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude brought powerful AI into everyday life. Suddenly, anyone with an internet connection could ask a computer to write, summarize, brainstorm, or create images.
    • This is the AI landscape you're stepping into now. The tools are powerful, accessible, and still evolving rapidly — which is exactly why this course exists.
Note

Notice the pattern: two periods of enormous excitement, each followed by a collapse in funding and interest when the technology couldn't deliver what was promised. Those are the "AI Winters," flagged above. Whether we're currently in another hype cycle is a genuinely open question — and a good one to keep in mind all semester.

Narrow AI vs. General AI — The Difference That Matters

One of the most important distinctions in AI literacy, and it comes up constantly.

Narrow AI

Exists today — every AI system you have ever used.

What it is: systems designed for specific tasks.

Examples: your phone's voice assistant, Netflix's recommendation engine, your email spam filter, a chess engine, a medical-imaging classifier.

The limit: incredibly good at one job — sometimes better than humans — but can't step outside it. A chess-playing AI can't write an essay. A medical-diagnosis AI can't drive a car.

General AI (AGI)

Does not yet exist. Hypothetical.

What it would be: a system with human-level, cross-domain reasoning ability.

What it could do: learn any intellectual task a human can learn, switch between domains, and apply knowledge from one area to another.

The status: no researcher has achieved it, and there's serious debate about whether or when it might be possible.

Note

Why this matters: When you read headlines like "AI Can Now Do X," the system being described is narrow AI — excellent at one thing, not a thinking machine. Understanding this distinction helps you evaluate AI claims critically and avoid both hype and fear.

AI in Everyday Applications — It's Already Around You

You probably interact with AI multiple times every day without realizing it.

Five places AI is already working for you
Smart Assistants
Siri, Google Assistant, and Alexa use AI to understand your voice, interpret your requests, and provide answers or take actions. They're narrow AI systems specialized in language understanding and task execution.
Recommendation Systems
Netflix suggests shows you might like. Spotify creates playlists based on your listening habits. Amazon recommends products. All of these use ML models that learn patterns from your behavior and the behavior of millions of other users.
Search Engines
When you search Google, AI helps rank results by relevance, understand the intent behind your query, and even generate direct answers. The search experience you use is one of the most sophisticated AI applications in daily life.
Email Filtering
Your email's spam filter is a classic ML application — it learns from millions of labeled examples to decide which messages are legitimate and which are not.
Navigation and Maps
Google Maps and Waze use AI to predict traffic patterns, estimate arrival times, and suggest optimal routes in real time.
Photo Organization
Your phone's photo app can group pictures by people, places, or events using AI-powered image recognition — another narrow AI system doing one job very well.
