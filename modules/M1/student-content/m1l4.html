Where does AI get its data?

Every AI system runs on data. The models you interact with — from search engines to chatbots — were trained on massive collections of text, images, audio, and other information gathered from the real world. But that data doesn't appear magically. Someone, somewhere, collected it. And the way it was collected shapes what the AI can do, what it gets wrong, and whose interests it serves.

In this lesson, you'll learn how big data is gathered, where bias sneaks into datasets, and what privacy and consent frameworks exist to protect people. This matters because garbage in, garbage out — if the training data is biased or incomplete, the AI system will be biased or incomplete too. You can't evaluate AI output critically without understanding its data origins.

Big Data Collection Methods — How Is Training Data Gathered?

Five pathways feed the pool that models learn from.

Open this section: the five ways training data is collected

AI models need enormous amounts of data to learn. Here are the main ways that data is collected:

Web Crawling and Scraping

Automated programs (called "crawlers" or "scrapers") browse the internet and collect text, images, and metadata from websites, forums, social media platforms, and news sites. This is how large language models like Gemini and ChatGPT gather much of their training text — by reading billions of web pages. Companies don't always ask permission before scraping content, which raises legal and ethical questions about ownership and consent.

Public Datasets and Open Data

Governments, universities, and organizations publish datasets for research and public use. Examples include census data, medical records (anonymized), scientific publications, and government reports. These are valuable because they're curated and documented — but they can still reflect the biases of whoever collected and published them.

User-Generated Content

Every time you post on social media, write a review, upload a photo, or interact with an app, you're generating data that could be used to train AI systems. Some platforms include data collection in their terms of service; others are less transparent. The scale is staggering: billions of posts, comments, and images every day.

Commercial Data Purchases

Companies buy datasets from data brokers — businesses that collect, aggregate, and sell personal information. These datasets can include everything from purchasing habits to location history. The people whose data is being sold often don't know it's happening.

Sensors and IoT Devices

Cameras, microphones, wearables, and smart devices continuously generate data. Traffic cameras feed computer vision models. Smart speakers collect voice data. Fitness trackers produce health data. This data is collected passively — you don't actively "give" it; it's captured as you go about your day.

Note

Key point: The more diverse and representative the training data, the fairer and more accurate the AI system tends to be. But collecting truly representative data at scale is extremely difficult — and that's where bias enters the picture.

Bias Sources in Datasets — Where Does Bias Come From?

Two ways it gets in: who ends up in the data, and who decides what the labels mean.

Open this section: sampling bias and labeling bias

Bias in AI doesn't come from the algorithm being "prejudiced." It comes from the data. Here are the two main sources:

Sampling Bias — Whose Voice Is Missing?

Sampling bias happens when the data used to train a model doesn't represent the full population the model will serve. If you train a facial recognition system mostly on photos of light-skinned people, it will perform poorly on dark-skinned faces. The algorithm isn't racist — but its output is discriminatory because its training data was unrepresentative.

Real-world examples:

  • Voice assistants that struggle with certain accents or dialects because the voice data used to train them came mostly from speakers with standard accents.
  • Medical diagnosis AI trained primarily on data from one demographic group, leading to less accurate predictions for underrepresented groups.
  • Hiring algorithms trained on historical hiring data from a company that mostly hired men — the system learns to favor male candidates because that's what "success" looked like in the training data.

Why it happens: It's easier and cheaper to collect data from certain populations. English-language web content vastly outnumbers content in other languages. Urban areas have more sensors and cameras than rural areas. Tech companies' user bases skew younger, wealthier, and more tech-savvy than the general population.

Labeling Bias — Who Decides What's "Correct"?

Labeling bias happens when the people (or systems) who label training data introduce their own assumptions, blind spots, or cultural biases into the labels. Remember from M1L3: in supervised learning, someone has to tell the system what the "correct" answer is for each training example. If those answers are biased, the model learns bias.

Real-world examples:

  • Content moderation AI trained on labels created by moderators from one cultural background may flag language that's normal or respectful in other cultures as "offensive."
  • Sentiment analysis tools that misread sarcasm or culturally specific expressions because the labelers didn't recognize them.
  • Image classification datasets where objects associated with certain professions are labeled in stereotypical ways (e.g., "nurse" images are mostly women, "doctor" images are mostly men).

Why it happens: Human labelers bring their own perspectives and blind spots. Labeling guidelines may be vague or culturally specific. When labeling is outsourced to low-wage workers (common in the industry), those workers may not have the context or training to make nuanced decisions. Rushed labeling to meet deadlines increases errors.

Note

Why this matters: When you evaluate AI-generated content, one of your first questions should be: What data was this system trained on, and whose perspectives might be missing? You can't assess bias in the output without considering bias in the input.

Privacy and Consent Frameworks — Protecting People Behind the Data

What exists to protect the people the data came from.

Open this section: consent, anonymization, minimization, GDPR and CCPA

When billions of data points are collected to train AI systems, privacy becomes a major concern. Here are the key frameworks and concepts:

Informed Consent

The gold standard for ethical data collection: people know what data is being collected, why it's being collected, how it will be used, and they explicitly agree to it. In practice, informed consent is rare in big-data AI. Most web scraping happens without the knowledge of the people whose content is being collected. Terms-of-service agreements are long, complex, and rarely read — which raises questions about whether consent given through them is truly "informed."

Anonymization and De-identification

Data can be stripped of direct identifiers (name, email, address) before being used for training. But anonymization is harder than it sounds: researchers have repeatedly re-identified "anonymous" individuals by cross-referencing datasets. True de-identification requires careful techniques and ongoing review.

Data Minimization

The principle that you should collect only the data you actually need, not everything available. If an app needs your location to give you directions, it shouldn't also be collecting your contacts list and browsing history. Data minimization reduces privacy risk — but many AI systems are built on the opposite principle: collect as much as possible, figure out the use later.

Regulatory Frameworks

GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) is Europe's comprehensive privacy law. It gives individuals rights over their data, including the right to access it, correct it, and request deletion (the "right to be forgotten"). It requires explicit consent for data processing, and it applies to any organization handling EU residents' data, regardless of where the organization is located.

CCPA (California Consumer Privacy Act) is California's privacy law, similar in spirit to GDPR but with different specifics. It gives California residents rights to know what personal data is collected, request deletion, and opt out of data sales.

These frameworks create legal obligations for companies — but enforcement varies, and much AI training data was collected before these laws existed. For what the individual tools you use say they do with what you type, the course keeps a cross-tool comparison: How the tools handle your data.

Note

Tension to understand: AI development pushes toward collecting more data (bigger data = better models). Privacy frameworks push toward collecting less data and protecting individual rights. Navigating this tension is one of the central ethical challenges in AI today.

Analyze a Dataset for Representation Gaps

About 25 minutes Activity M1L4-A1 · ungraded

This activity is the foundation for your M1-Q02 assessment (Dataset bias/representation analysis, due Sunday 9/6). You'll use an AI tool to help analyze a real-world dataset scenario and identify representation gaps.

Step-by-step instructions:

  1. Open Gemini in a browser tab (or another tool if you prefer). Step-by-step guides for the two tools the college provides are here if you want one: Gemini and Microsoft Copilot.
  2. Copy and paste this prompt into it: 
    I want to analyze a dataset for bias and representation gaps. Imagine a dataset of 10,000 news articles used to train an AI summarization tool. The dataset was collected by scraping the front pages of 50 major English-language news websites over one year. For each article, the dataset includes: headline, full text, publication date, source website, and topic category. Help me identify potential representation gaps and bias sources in this dataset. Think about: whose voices might be missing? What topics might be underrepresented? What languages, regions, or perspectives are excluded? What kinds of sampling bias and labeling bias could be present?
  3. Read the response carefully. Look for at least four distinct bias or representation concerns it identifies.
  4. Now follow up with this second prompt to push deeper: 
    For each of the top four bias concerns you identified, suggest one practical mitigation strategy — something a data scientist could do to make the dataset more representative and fair.
  5. Record your findings in a document (Google Doc, Word, or the notes area below in the SPA). Your notes should include:
    • The four bias/representation concerns you identified
    • One mitigation strategy per concern
    • A brief reflection (2–3 sentences): How might these biases affect the AI summarization tool's output? Whose interests could be harmed?
  6. This work feeds directly into your M1-Q02 assessment. You'll expand and formalize it during the week-3 studio session. Save your notes — you'll need them.
