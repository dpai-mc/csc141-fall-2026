Integrity Is About Trust, Not Just Rules

When you hand in work, you’re making a promise.

Read this section

“Academic integrity” can sound like a list of things you’ll get in trouble for. It’s more useful to think of it as a promise.

When you hand in work, you’re telling your instructor: this shows what I can do. A grade is only worth something because that promise is usually kept — to you, to the next school that reads your transcript, to the employer who assumes your certificate means you can do the job. When the promise breaks, the damage isn’t only to the person who broke it. It makes everyone’s work a little harder to believe.

AI tools didn’t create that problem. They made it much easier to blur — because an AI tool can produce work that looks like yours in seconds, and because the line between “help” and “doing it for you” is genuinely harder to see than it was with a copied essay.

The course’s definition:

Academic integrity (with AI). The honest and transparent use of AI tools in academic work, including proper disclosure, citation, and adherence to institutional policies on AI-assisted work.

Three parts: disclose what the AI did, cite what you took from it, and follow the rules of the course you’re in. The rest of this lesson takes them one at a time.

Who’s the Author?

You are responsible for everything you submit — and citing isn’t the same as disclosing.

Read this section

Here’s the part that doesn’t change no matter which rules apply: when you submit work, you are responsible for all of it — including every sentence, fact and source an AI tool produced for you. “The AI wrote that” is an explanation, never an excuse.

That’s why the difference between two words matters:

  • Citing means crediting the AI for specific content you used — a sentence you quoted, a summary you relied on, an image it made. Citation says where this piece came from.
  • Disclosing means stating how you used AI in the process — brainstorming, outlining, checking grammar, translating — even when none of its words ended up in your work. Disclosure says what help you had.

You can need one, the other, or both. Quoting an AI’s explanation needs a citation. Using AI to brainstorm topics needs a disclosure. Using it to brainstorm and quoting a line from it needs both.

A good disclosure is specific. Compare:

“I used AI on this assignment.”

“I used Gemini to suggest possible topics and to check grammar in my final draft. I chose the topic, did the research, and wrote every sentence myself.”

The first one is technically a disclosure. The second one actually tells the reader something.

How to Credit AI

Three style guides, the same basics, different details.

Read this section

The three style guides you’re most likely to meet in college have each published guidance on citing generative AI. They agree on the basics — name the tool and who makes it, give the date, show where it came from — and differ on the details.

Crediting generative AI in three style guides
StyleWhere the credit goesShape of the entryExample
APA A reference-list entry, plus an in-text citation — and a sentence in your text saying how you used the tool. Company. (Year). Tool name [Description]. URL Google. (2026). Gemini [Large language model]. https://gemini.google.com — in text: (Google, 2026)
MLA A Works Cited entry for content you quote or paraphrase; other uses (editing, translating) acknowledged in a note or in your text. “Description of your prompt” prompt. Tool name, version (if shown), Company, date you used it, URL. “Explain how a bill becomes law in California” prompt. Gemini, Google, 28 Sept. 2026, gemini.google.com.
Chicago A footnote or endnote. Whether it also needs a bibliography entry varies — check with your instructor. Tool name, response to “your prompt,” Company, date. 1. Gemini, response to “Explain how a bill becomes law in California,” Google, September 28, 2026.

Citation formats accurate as of 2026-09. Style guides are still revising their AI guidance. Sources: MLA Style Center, How do I cite generative AI in MLA style? (revised August 2025) · APA Style blog, Citing generative AI in APA Style: Part 1 — Reference formats · Chicago Manual of Style, 18th ed., §14.112 (summarized by McMaster University Library).

Two things all three agree on that are easy to forget:

  • Say what you asked. Most of these formats include your prompt, or a description of it. An AI’s answer depends on the question, so the question is part of the source.
  • Say it in your text too. A citation at the end isn’t enough if the reader can’t tell which parts of your work came from the AI. Introduce quoted AI text the way you’d introduce any quotation.

And the rule under all the formats: your instructor’s syllabus decides whether AI output can be used at all. A perfect citation doesn’t make a use acceptable if the course didn’t allow it.

Verify

Before you cite AI in a real assignment, check two things: what your instructor’s syllabus says about AI, and which style guide the course uses. If you can’t find either, ask. Then check the style guide’s own current page — this table is a snapshot.

What the Rules Actually Say

There isn’t one rule for every class — on purpose.

Read this section

If you’re hoping for one rule that covers every class, there isn’t one — and that’s on purpose.

California’s community colleges get guidance on this from the Academic Senate for California Community Colleges, the statewide body that represents faculty on academic matters. Its paper Academic Integrity Policies in the Age of Artificial Intelligence, adopted in spring 2024, explicitly isn’t a mandate. It leaves the decision to individual instructors and disciplines, and offers sample syllabus statements running from fully open to fully closed.

So the same use of AI can be expected in one class and prohibited in the next. That’s not inconsistency — a writing course, a lab course and a computer science course have genuinely different things they’re trying to teach.

In practice, the rule that applies to you is the one in each course’s syllabus. If a syllabus doesn’t say, ask your instructor before you use AI on an assignment — not after. “I didn’t know” is a much weaker position than “I asked.”

Where do you land? You’ll be asked in the activity, in one sentence. There’s no right answer — people who teach for a living disagree about this. What matters is that you can say why.

Judging a Case

The clearest real case so far happened in a federal court.

Read this section

The clearest real case so far didn’t happen in a classroom. It happened in a federal court.

In 2023, in a lawsuit called Mata v. Avianca, two New York lawyers filed a brief that cited court decisions that didn’t exist — ChatGPT had produced them, and nobody checked. When the judge questioned the cases, the lawyers stood by them instead of saying where they’d come from. In June 2023 the court fined the lawyers and their firm $5,000 and ordered them to write to the judges falsely named as authors of the fake opinions. (summary of the court’s sanctions order)

The court’s problem wasn’t that they used an AI tool. It was that they submitted work they hadn’t checked, as if they had, and then weren’t honest about it. Swap “brief” for “essay” and “judge” for “instructor” and it’s the same three failures this lesson is about: no verification, no disclosure, and responsibility dodged.

Most real situations are much less dramatic, and much harder to call. Three questions work for any case:

  1. What did the AI actually do? Brainstorm, edit, translate, draft, write the whole thing?
  2. What did the rules for that course allow?
  3. What did the person say about it? Nothing? Something vague? Something specific?

Notice what’s not on the list: how the writing sounds.

Caution

You can’t reliably tell AI writing by reading it — and neither can the tools that claim to. In 2023, OpenAI withdrew its own AI-text detector because it wasn’t accurate enough, and a Stanford study found that popular detectors regularly flagged essays by non-native English writers as AI-written. Judging a case by how the writing “sounds” gets real people wrongly accused. Judge what was done and what was said.

M3L3-A1 — Three cases

30 minutes Not submitted

Three students answered the same assignment. Decide whether each one met the course’s AI rule — and say why.

Short response (150–200 words): Should community colleges require every student to take a personal-finance course? Take a position and give your strongest reason.

This course’s AI rule (from the syllabus): AI tools may be used for brainstorming and for grammar and spelling checks. All ideas and wording in your submitted work must be your own. Any AI use must be disclosed in a note at the end of your response.

Step 1 — read each sample and its process notes

The notes tell you what the student actually did. That’s your evidence — not how the writing sounds.

Step 2 — for each sample, make three calls

Using the course’s rule above.

Step 3 — say why

Two or three sentences per sample.

Step 4 — where do you land?

One sentence: where on the spectrum would you put the line for a course like this one, and why?

Note

About these samples. Jordan, Priya and Sam are fictional. Their responses and process notes were drafted for this course with the help of an AI tool and then reviewed and edited by your instructor — which is exactly the kind of statement this lesson is asking you to be able to write.

I think community colleges should require a personal-finance course, because most of us are already making money decisions that can follow us for years. I’m twenty and I already have a car loan, a credit card, and a part-time job that pays into a retirement account I don’t understand. Nobody taught me any of it. I learned what an interest rate really costs by paying one. A single required course wouldn’t make everyone an expert, and it wouldn’t need to be long, but it would mean students hear the basics — budgeting, credit, loans, taxes — before a mistake teaches them the expensive way. Some people will say it’s one more requirement in a schedule that’s already full, and that’s fair. But a community college is supposed to prepare people for real life, not just transfer, and money is part of real life for every single student here. It would probably be the class people use most.

  • Asked a friend to read it; changed one sentence after.
  • Used no AI tools.

Disclosure note at the end of the submission: (none)

Community colleges should require a personal-finance course because financial mistakes made early can take years to fix. Many students are managing rent, loans, or credit cards for the first time while they’re in school, and most never had a class that explained how any of it works. The strongest reason is fairness: students whose families talk openly about money start with an advantage that others don’t have, and a required course is one of the few ways a college can close that gap. Critics argue that students can learn this online for free, and that’s true — but “can” isn’t “will,” and the students who need it most are often the least likely to go looking. A required course reaches everyone, including the students who would never have signed up for it on their own. A short, practical course on budgeting, credit and debt would pay off long after graduation.

Process notes

  • Asked Gemini: “Give me five reasons colleges might require a personal finance class.” Picked the fairness reason from its list and wrote the response herself.
  • Pasted her finished draft into Gemini and asked it to “fix grammar and spelling only, don’t change my words.” Accepted two comma fixes and one spelling fix.

Disclosure note at the end of the submission: “I used AI to help with this.”

Requiring a personal-finance course at community colleges would be a meaningful investment in student success. Financial literacy equips learners with essential skills such as budgeting, managing credit, and understanding loans — competencies that directly influence long-term well-being. Moreover, many community college students are already navigating significant financial responsibilities, making this knowledge immediately applicable. Research consistently highlights that students with strong financial knowledge are better positioned to make informed decisions regarding tuition, employment, and borrowing. While some may argue that adding a requirement could delay degree completion, the course could be designed as a flexible, low-unit option. Additionally, integrating real-world scenarios, such as creating a monthly budget or comparing loan offers, would ensure that the content remains relevant, engaging, and directly connected to students’ everyday lives. Ultimately, the strongest reason to require such a course is its potential to reduce financial stress, which is a leading factor in students leaving college. By empowering students with practical knowledge, community colleges can foster both academic persistence and lifelong financial stability.
