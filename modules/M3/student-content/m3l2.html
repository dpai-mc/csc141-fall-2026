Three Jobs AI Does for Learners

Writing, studying and getting organized — each with a version that helps and a version that doesn’t.

Read this section

Last lesson was about using AI to find things out. This one is about using it to learn them — to write, to study, and to keep your life organized enough to do both. Most of what students actually use AI for falls into three jobs.

Writing help

Where it helps: getting unstuck on a first draft, seeing your own paragraph restructured, adjusting tone for a different reader, catching grammar and spelling.

Where it goes wrong: the draft stops being yours — your ideas, your voice, and your practice at putting them into words all quietly move to the tool.

Study help

Where it helps: explaining a concept a different way, turning your notes into practice questions or flashcards, talking a topic through like a patient tutor.

Where it goes wrong: reading a clear explanation feels like understanding it. It isn’t — until you can explain it yourself.

Getting organized

Where it helps: breaking a big assignment into steps, turning a messy week into a plan, tidying scattered notes into something you can find again.

Where it goes wrong: a plan you didn’t make is easy to ignore — and it can’t see the parts of your week you didn’t tell it about.

Every one of those jobs has a version that makes you better at something and a version that means you never have to get better at it. The tool doesn’t know which one you’re doing. You do.

Helping You Learn vs. Doing the Learning

Offloading is normal. The question is what you offload.

Read this section

There’s a name for handing a mental task to a tool: cognitive offloading — letting something outside your head do thinking you’d otherwise do yourself. It isn’t new and it isn’t bad. You offload arithmetic to a calculator, phone numbers to your contacts, and directions to a map app, and your life is better for it.

The question is never whether you offload. It’s what. If the thing you hand over is the thing you’re in the class to learn, you’ve skipped the class while still sitting in it.

That matters because of a second idea: skill atrophy — skills you stop practicing fade. Nobody memorizes phone numbers any more, and for phone numbers that’s fine. It’s less fine for writing a clear paragraph, working through a problem, or remembering what you studied long enough to use it.

There’s now direct evidence of how this plays out. In a study published in 2025, researchers gave nearly a thousand high-school math students in Turkey access to an AI tool during practice. Students with a plain chat version did much better on practice problems while they had it — and then did worse on the exam than students who’d never had it at all, once it was taken away. A second group used a version set up to act like a tutor, giving hints instead of answers. They improved in practice too, and the harm to their exam scores largely disappeared.

The tool didn’t change what they could look up. It changed who did the thinking.

Helps you learn

  • “Quiz me on this — one question at a time.”
  • “Explain this, then let me explain it back.”
  • “Tell me what’s weak in my draft.”
  • “Give me a hint for step two.”
  • “Help me break this assignment into steps.”

Does the learning for you

  • “Give me the answers to these questions.”
  • “Explain this.” (and stop there)
  • “Rewrite my draft.”
  • “Solve this.”
  • “Do this assignment.”

Same tool, same topic. The difference is who does the thinking.

The honest test is simple: could you do it without the tool next time? If yes, the AI helped you learn. If you’d need it again for the same thing, it did the work — which might be exactly what you wanted, but then be honest with yourself about which one it was.

Caution

Feeling like you learned something isn’t the same as learning it. A clear explanation, a tidy summary or a finished draft all produce the feeling. The only way to find out is to try without the tool — which is exactly what this lesson’s activity asks you to do.

Tutor, Not Ghostwriter

Three prompt moves that make the tool make you think.

Read this section

You already know how to write a good prompt — that was Module 2. The same skills turn an AI tool from something that does the work into something that makes you do it. Three moves do most of the job.

Ask for questions, not answers. Have it quiz you, and make it wait.

I'm studying [topic] for [class]. Quiz me with one question at a time. Wait for my answer before you tell me if I'm right, and if I'm wrong, give me a hint before you give me the answer.

Ask for hints, not solutions. When you’re stuck, ask for the smallest nudge that gets you moving.

I'm stuck on this problem: [paste it]. Don't solve it. Give me the smallest hint that would help me take the next step, and then stop.

Ask it to find problems, not fix them. Keep the pen in your hand.

Here's my draft: [paste it]. Point out the three biggest weaknesses and explain why each one is a weakness. Don't rewrite anything — I'll make the changes myself.

Two things to keep in mind whichever way you use it. Explanations, practice questions and summaries can be wrong — check anything you’re going to rely on, the way you did in M3L1. And study material you paste in (a classmate’s notes, your instructor’s slides, anything with someone’s personal information) goes wherever that tool’s account terms send it; M1L5’s rule about which account you’re using applies here too. How to credit AI help in work you hand in is next, in M3L3.

Try this

Use the first prompt on something you have a test on soon. Ten minutes of being quizzed — and getting some wrong — will do more for the test than an hour of re-reading a summary.

A Study Tool Built From Your Own Sources

The same four steps from M3L1 — except you choose what goes in.

Read this section

In M3L1 you saw the four steps an app takes when it looks something up. A study tool built from your own sources runs the same steps with one change: you choose the sources. You add your notes, a reading, a textbook chapter — and the tool answers, quizzes and summarizes from those, instead of from the open web.

For studying, that’s a real advantage. The questions come from the material you’ll actually be tested on, and when something isn’t in your sources, a well-built tool says so instead of filling the gap.

Your college account includes a tool built for exactly this: Gemini Notebook (formerly NotebookLM), which M3L1 introduced. How to get around it is in the Gemini Notebook guide and shown in class; this lesson is about using it well.

What “your own sources” buys you

  • Questions and flashcards about your course, not the internet’s version of it.
  • Answers you can trace back to a page you added.
  • A tool that can say “that isn’t in your sources” — which is useful information.

What it doesn’t

  • It doesn’t make the answers right. A tool can still misread a source or attach a claim to the wrong page. Check it the way you did in M3L1.
  • It’s only as good as what you put in — and blind to anything you left out.
  • It doesn’t do the learning. A generated quiz you never take, or a summary you only skim, is M3L2’s offloading problem with a nicer interface.

What to put in. Your own notes are always fine, and so are readings you were given to study. Instructor slides and handouts: only if your instructor says it’s OK to upload them. Never a classmate’s work, and never anything with someone’s personal information — M1L5’s rule about whose account you’re using applies here too, and the privacy guide says what happens to what you upload.

Read one student’s take

A computer science student and tech writer compared four AI tools as study partners for XDA Developers, in March 2026: I tested NotebookLM, Gemini, Claude, and ChatGPT for studying, and one stood out immediately (about a ten-minute read). She makes the same point this section does — that a tool working only from your sources “will simply tell you that your sources don’t mention it” — and she also ranks the tools. Read it, then think through these four questions:

  1. What was actually tested? The headline says “tested.” Look for the test: the same materials in each tool? A measure of what she learned? Or one person’s experience over time?
  2. Which claims are about design, and which are rankings? “It generates flashcards only from the content included in your sources” describes how a tool is built. “Simply doesn’t compare to the rest” is a verdict. Which kind is still useful a year from now?
  3. Which of her study habits would pass the learning test? She describes listening to generated podcasts as “study sessions without even trying,” and taking a quiz as “immediate proof.” Hold both up against this lesson’s test.
  4. Does “only from your sources” mean “always right”? What would you still have to check?
Verify

Rankings of AI tools come with a date and a tester, whether they say so or not. This one is one student, her own materials, in March 2026 — and the tool she picked has been renamed and changed since. That’s why this course doesn’t rank tools: it teaches you to check what a ranking actually measured.

M3L2-A1 — Build a study notebook

35 minutes Not submitted

Build a small study tool from your own sources for a class you’re taking, use it, and find out whether it helped you learn.

Step 1 — pick a class and a topic

Something you’ll be tested on or have to write about soon. An item from your 10 Things list works too.

Step 2 — gather two or three sources

Your own notes, a reading you were given, a chapter you’re studying. Only things you’re allowed to upload — see “What to put in” above.

Step 3 — pick your route

All three routes count the same. Use whichever your account allows.

  • Route A — a Gemini notebook. Create a notebook and add your sources to it.
  • Route B — a Gemini chat with your files. Open Gemini (or another tool if you prefer), start a new chat and upload your sources to it.
  • Route C — a Gemini chat with your notes pasted in. Paste the text of your sources into a new chat. Nothing to upload.

Some notebook features are limited to students 18 and over, even on a college account, and notebooks are still rolling out to school accounts. Routes B and C teach exactly the same thing.

Step 4 — make it quiz you

Use this prompt, with your topic filled in:

Using only the sources I've added, quiz me on [topic], one question at a time. Wait for my answer. Then tell me whether I'm right, and point to the part of my sources that supports the answer.

Answer at least five questions.

Step 5 — check one answer

Pick one answer the tool marked as right. Find the place in your source that supports it — the M3L1 method.

Step 6 — the test

Close the chat or notebook. Without looking back at it, write two or three sentences that explain the topic from what you now know.

Note

“It did the work for me” is an honest answer, not a wrong one. Sometimes that’s what you wanted. The point is knowing which one happened.

Step 7 — your verdict

Did it help you learn, do the work for you, or some of both? And one thing you’d change next time. Keep your notebook or chat — next week’s studio builds on it.
