What a Personal AI Code Is

A personal policy, in four parts.

Read this section

Personal AI Code of Conduct. A self-authored set of guidelines governing how an individual chooses to use AI tools responsibly, ethically, and effectively — developed and refined throughout this course.

It isn’t a pledge, and it isn’t a list of banned tools. It’s closer to a personal policy: what you’ll do, what you won’t, what you’ll tell people, and when you’ll stop and reconsider. It has four parts.

Purpose statement

What it answers: why you use AI at all, and what you’re protecting.

Example line: “I use AI to get unstuck and to check my work — not to skip the parts of a class that are there to teach me something.”

Guidelines

What it answers: when you will and won’t use AI, specifically.

Example line: “In any class that allows it, I’ll have AI quiz me before tests — and I won’t paste in a question from a graded assignment.”

Disclosure policy

What it answers: what you tell people about your AI use, and how.

Example line: “Any time AI helped with work I hand in, I add a one-line note saying which tool and what it did.”

Review plan

What it answers: when you’ll look at your Code again, and what would make you change it.

Example line: “I’ll reread this at the start of every semester, and any time I’m not sure whether a use crosses a line.”

What Makes a Guideline Good

Specific enough that you’d know, in the moment, whether you were following it.

Read this section

The difference between a Code that does something and one that doesn’t is almost all in the guidelines. A good guideline is specific enough that you’d know, in the moment, whether you were following it.

Vague

  • “Use AI responsibly.”
  • “Don’t cheat with AI.”
  • “Always verify.”
  • “Protect my privacy.”
  • “Use AI to learn.”

Actionable

  • “Before I use AI on an assignment, I check the syllabus — and if it doesn’t say, I ask.”
  • “I don’t paste a graded question into an AI tool unless the assignment says I may.”
  • “Any fact from an AI tool that goes into work I hand in, I check in a source I’ve opened myself.”
  • “I don’t put other people’s names, grades or personal details into any AI tool.”
  • “When I’m studying, I ask AI to quiz me or give hints — not to give me the answers.”

The test: could someone watching you tell whether you’d followed it?

Cover more than school. Your Code should reach at least three kinds of situation: academic work (your classes), personal learning (things you’re figuring out for yourself — your 10 Things list is full of them), and professional use (a job you have now or want later). The same rule can look different in each: a use one instructor forbids may be exactly what an employer expects.

Build in triggers. A review plan works best with a trigger, not just a date — “when I start a new job,” “when a course’s AI rule surprises me,” “when I catch myself not checking.”

Caution

A Code you’d never break isn’t doing anything. If every guideline describes what you already do without thinking, it isn’t a Code yet — it’s a description. At least one line should ask something of you.

The Study Tool

Built from your sources, checked, tested on yourself, and all of it disclosed.

Read this section

The other half of this week puts all three of last week’s moves into one piece of work. You’ll build a study tool for a class you’re taking from sources you chose — the notebook or chat from M3L2’s activity is a head start — check what it tells you against those sources, use it, and then find out without it whether it taught you anything. Along the way you cite your sources and keep a log of every AI interaction.

The learning test is the heart of it — the part that tells you whether the tool taught you anything.

The log isn’t paperwork. It’s what makes the disclosure honest, because you can’t disclose what you didn’t keep track of.
