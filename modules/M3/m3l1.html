The Model and the App Around It

When you use an AI tool, you’re using a product — not talking to a model directly.

Read this section

Module 3 is about using AI to learn — to research, to study, to write — without fooling yourself about what it just did. That starts with a distinction almost nobody makes out loud, even though every tool you have used this term depends on it.

When you use “Gemini” or “ChatGPT,” you are not talking to a model directly. You are using a product.

AI product vs. underlying model. A model generates text from its trained parameters plus whatever is in its context; it has no connection to anything else. An AI product (the Gemini app, the ChatGPT app, a third-party app built on an API) is that model with software wrapped around it — software that can call external tools, including web search, on the model’s behalf. Most things students assume are properties of “the AI” are actually properties of the product: web access, file uploads, memory, image generation. The same model reached through a different product behaves differently.

Read the first sentence of that definition again, because the rest of this lesson hangs on it: the model reaches nothing. It has what it learned in training, and it has what is in front of it right now — your question, the conversation so far, and anything the app put there. That is the whole list.

The mismatch you were asked to hold onto. In M2L1 you asked a tool to describe what kind of model produced its answer, and its description didn’t quite line up with the lesson. Here is why. The model can’t look at itself. When you asked, the app answered the way it answers everything: from what the model absorbed in training, plus whatever instructions the product wraps around your conversation. So a self-description is a mix of general knowledge about AI and what the product was set up to say — not a report from the inside. That isn’t the tool lying. It’s the tool doing the only thing it can, which is producing likely text. It is also your first example of the habit this module is about: a confident answer is not evidence of how it was made.

Caution

“The AI said” hides two different things. When you tell someone what an AI tool told you, it’s worth knowing whether you’re reporting what a model produced from memory or what an app found and handed it. They fail differently — and the rest of this lesson is about telling them apart.

How a Web-Connected Answer Gets Made

If the model reaches nothing, the app does the reaching — in four steps.

Read this section

If the model reaches nothing, how does an AI tool ever tell you about something that happened last week?

The app does the reaching. When an answer draws on current web pages, four things happened, usually in a second or two:

  1. The app decided this question needs fresh information. Sometimes the product decides; sometimes the model is prompted to make that call. Either way, it’s a decision, made per question.
  2. The app sent a query out to a search service.
  3. The results — links, snippets, sometimes whole pages — were put into the model’s context, right alongside your question, the same way your typed words are.
  4. The model wrote an answer from that material, ideally saying which source each part came from.

Notice what did not happen: the model never went online. Someone handed it documents, and it read them. That is all retrieval is. The course’s name for it:

Retrieval-grounded generation (search-grounded). Answering by fetching sources at question time and putting them into the model’s context, so it writes from those documents rather than from parameters alone. The model does not “go online” — the surrounding product queries a search service and hands the results to the model as context. Used selectively: the system typically decides per question whether a lookup is warranted. The consequence that matters here: a citation shows you where to check, not that anyone checked.

So “can it search?” is a question about the product, not the AI. The same underlying model might look things up in a company’s consumer app, not look anything up when a developer reaches it directly, and look things up only sometimes in someone else’s app built on top of it. And inside one app it is usually selective — the app decides per question whether a lookup is worth it, and it may not tell you which way it went.

Some products are built to look things up by default and put their sources front and center. There’s a name for that design:

Answer engine. A product designed retrieval-first — it searches by default and puts sources in the foreground (Perplexity is the usual example). A design posture of a product, not a capability boundary between models. Useful for describing a product’s default behaviour; useless for predicting whether any given answer was looked up.

When you choose the sources. Some tools are built to work the other way around. Instead of the app searching the web, you add the sources — your notes, a reading, a textbook chapter — and the app puts those into the model’s context. Same four steps, except step 2 is you. Your college account includes one built this way, Gemini Notebook (called NotebookLM until July 2026, so you’ll see both names online); M3L2 puts it to work. The rule doesn’t change: a citation to your own source still tells you where to check, not that anyone checked.

Product examples accurate as of 2026-09.

The upshot: you can’t predict any of this from a brand name, and you can’t assume it’s the same as last month. Every major AI assistant may look something up or may answer from memory, often deciding question by question. What you can do is read the answer itself for evidence — which is the next section.

Try this

Ask the same thing two ways. In one new chat, ask a question that obviously needs current information (“What happened in [your town] this week?”). In another new chat, ask a general-knowledge question (“How does a bill become a law?”). Watch whether the app shows a searching step or sources in each. It often won’t search the second time — which is the “decided per question” part, happening in front of you.

Reading the Answer for Evidence

Two kinds of answer, wrong in different ways — and two checklists for telling them apart.

Read this section

You will get two kinds of answer from AI tools, often without being told which one you got. Both can be right. Both can be wrong. They are wrong in different ways — and the retrieved kind is easier to miss.

When the answer came from recall. The model is producing likely text from what it absorbed in training. Most of the time that works surprisingly well. But when it doesn’t, the result looks exactly like when it does: fluent, specific, confident. A made-up statistic. A quotation nobody said. A book title with a plausible author and a plausible year that does not exist. When an AI tool states something false with the same confidence as something true, that is usually called a hallucination. In research, the classic version is the fabricated citation — a reference that looks perfect and leads nowhere.

When the answer came from retrieval. Now there are real sources, often with links you can click. That helps — genuinely. But it creates a subtler failure. The model can summarize a real page wrongly, attach a claim to the wrong source, or take a sentence out of the context that changed its meaning. A fabricated citation collapses the moment you look for it. A real, clickable, genuinely-existing source that simply doesn’t say what the summary claims will sail past a casual glance — because a link feels like verification. It isn’t.

A citation tells you where to check, not that anyone checked.

Two checklists do most of the work. The first tells you which kind of answer you probably got. The second names what went wrong, if something did. You’ll use both in this lesson’s activity, word for word.

Looked up, or recalled?

What it’s for: telling which kind of answer you probably got.

Signs it was looked up:

  • Named sources with links that work
  • Information newer than the model could have been trained on
  • A visible “searching” or “sources” step in the app

Signs it was recalled:

  • Confident specifics with no links
  • A citation written out as plain text you’d have to go and find yourself
  • None of the signs above

Can’t tell is an honest answer. Say what you’d need to see to decide.

What kind of wrong?

What it’s for: naming the problem, claim by claim.

  1. The source doesn’t exist
  2. A real source that doesn’t support the claim
  3. A claim with no source offered
  4. Accurate as stated

A claim with no source may still be true — it’s a risk, not a verdict. Check it anyway.

Verifying Like You Mean It

A big word for a small habit.

Read this section

Verification sounds like a big word for a small habit. Here is the habit.

Open the source. Not the snippet, not the preview — the page. If it’s long, use your browser’s find (Ctrl+F, or Cmd+F on a Mac) to jump to a key word from the claim.

Find the sentence. You’re looking for the part of the source that says the thing. If you can’t point to it, the source doesn’t support the claim — however relevant the page looks.

Check the claim, not the topic. A page about the right subject is not the same as a page that says the right thing. The most common real-world failure is a source that is about the claim without making it.

Check the date. A source can be real, relevant and correct — for two years ago.

Cross-check anything that matters with a second, independent source — ideally one close to the original: the agency that published the number, the study itself rather than an article about it, the organization’s own page.

Reverse-search a claim with no source. Take its most specific part — a number, a name, a quoted phrase — and search for it yourself. If it’s real, it usually turns up. If the only place it turns up is the AI’s answer, that’s your answer.

When you can’t open a source — a paywall, a login, a dead link — you haven’t learned whether it’s right. You’ve learned that you can’t check it. A dead link in particular is not proof the source never existed; pages move. Say so, and check something you can open instead.

Verify

Check one claim from any AI answer you get this week — even one you believe. Open the source, find the sentence. It takes two minutes, and it’s the only way to learn how often your tools are right about the things you ask them.

Using AI Well for Research

None of this means AI is bad at research. It means it’s a particular kind of help.

Read this section

None of this means AI is bad at research. It means it’s a particular kind of help.

Good uses

  • Getting oriented in a topic you know nothing about — the vocabulary, the main debates, what people argue about.
  • Brainstorming research questions and narrowing a topic that’s too big.
  • Finding places to start looking — search terms, kinds of sources, organizations that publish on the subject.
  • Summarizing a source you already have and have read yourself — so you can check the summary against it.
  • Stress-testing your own understanding — “explain this back to me,” “what’s the strongest objection to this.”

What it isn’t

  • It isn’t a source. You cite the article, not the chatbot’s description of the article. (How to credit the AI’s part in your work is next up this week.)
  • It isn’t a search engine, even when the app around it runs one. You get a written answer built from a selection of results someone else chose.
  • It isn’t a finished literature review. It gives you somewhere to start reading, not something to stop reading at.
  • It isn’t the last word on anything recent, contested, local, or specialized — which is most of what a research assignment is about.

Your college account gives you two tools that work for this: Gemini, and Gemini Notebook, which you’ll use in M3L2 — both come with your @student.mendocino.edu login. Copilot is available through the same address too. How to get around each one’s screens is shown in class and in the tool guides, not here.

Your 10 Things list is fair game. If an item on it involves finding something out — comparing options, understanding how something works, figuring out a process — that’s a research question, and it’s the kind this activity is built for.

One more thing you’ll start hearing about. A newer kind of tool doesn’t stop at answering questions about a web page — it takes actions in the browser on your behalf, clicking and filling things in for you. That is a different thing from anything in this lesson, and Module 6 comes back to it when the course looks at where AI is heading.

M3L1-A1 — Two tools, four claims

40 minutes Portfolio 07

Run one research question through two AI tools, then check four of their claims yourself.

Step 1 — pick a research question

Something you actually want to know, specific enough that a good answer would cite sources. An item from your 10 Things list works well. The question you choose appears in the PDF you file — so pick one you’re comfortable having your instructor read.

Step 2 — ask the first tool

Open Gemini (or another tool if you prefer) in a new chat and ask your question using the prompt below, with your question filled in. Copy the whole answer somewhere you can see it.

[Your question here]. Summarize the key findings in a few short paragraphs, and list the sources you used for each claim, with links where you have them.

Step 3 — ask a second tool the same thing

Use a second AI tool of your choice — Copilot, through your college account, works. Same prompt, new chat.

Step 4 — pick four claims

Two from each answer. Choose claims that are specific enough to check: a number, a date, a named finding, an “according to…”. Not the vague ones.

Step 5 — check each one

Open the source the tool gave (or reverse-search the claim if it gave none), find the sentence, and fill in the fields below. Use the two checklists from this lesson for the two drop-downs. If you can’t open a source — paywall, login, dead link — swap in a different claim and note the swap in the box for it. A dead link isn’t proof the source never existed.

Note

A result where everything checks out is a real result. So is one where both tools looked things up, or neither did. You’re not trying to catch a tool out. You’re finding out what each one actually did with your question.

Step 6 — close it out

Two or three sentences: did having citations in front of you make you more likely to trust the summary — and was that trust earned?

Check Yourself

Not graded, not recorded. Three questions.

Open the three questions
1. A friend says, “Gemini looked that up online for me.” Which restatement is most accurate?
2. An AI answer cites a real article with a working link. You open it and it’s about the right topic, but you can’t find anything in it that says what the answer claimed. What kind of wrong is this?
3. You ask the same app two questions on the same afternoon. One answer shows sources; the other shows none. What’s the most likely explanation?
