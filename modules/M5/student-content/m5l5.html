The Mini-Project in Five Steps

A real problem in your field, an AI solution, and an honest look at it.

Read this section

Module 5 ends with a short proposal: a real problem in your field, and how AI could help solve it. You won’t build anything. You’ll design it carefully enough that someone in your field could judge whether it’s worth trying. It follows five steps, and each step uses something you’ve already learned.

  1. Name the problem. A real problem or inefficiency in your field — specific enough that someone could tell whether it had been solved. Who has the problem, how often, and what it costs them.
  2. Design the solution. What the AI does, what people do, and the workflow that connects them — M5L2’s shape: a person starts it, the AI does a step, a person checks, a person decides.
  3. Analyze the ethics. Judge your own solution through at least one of M4L5’s three lenses, and look at fairness, privacy, transparency and accountability.
  4. Sketch the implementation. What you’d need to try it: the data, the tools you could actually reach, the people, a small pilot, and how you’d know whether it worked.
  5. Reflect on the limits. What your proposal can’t do, what could go wrong, and what it would take to move from proposal to practice.

What makes a problem statement strong

Weak

Healthcare has too much paperwork, and AI could fix it.

Too broad to solve, no one in particular has the problem, and it starts with the answer.

Strong

At a small family clinic, nurses spend the first hour of each shift reading the patient-portal messages that came in overnight and deciding which need a same-day reply. Urgent messages can wait behind routine ones until someone gets to them.

Specific people, a specific task, a cost you could measure — and no mention of AI yet.

Note

Start with the problem, not the tool. If you start from “what could AI do here?”, you’ll find a use for it whether or not anyone needs one. Start from what’s actually hard in the work. Sometimes the honest answer is that AI is only part of the fix — and saying so is part of a strong proposal.

Four Examples, Four Fields

The same five steps, in very different work.

Read this section

These are sketches to show the shape, not models to copy — each one compressed to a line per step. Yours will be your own field’s, and longer.

Healthcare — a message-sorting assistant

Problem
Overnight patient messages wait for a nurse to sort them, and urgent ones can sit behind routine ones.
Solution
The AI sorts messages into urgent, routine and administrative, and drafts replies to routine ones. A nurse reviews every urgent flag and every reply before it’s sent; messages mentioning certain symptoms skip the AI and go straight to a nurse.
Ethics
Harms: a missed urgent message. Fairness: patients who write in another language or very informally may be sorted wrongly more often. Rights: patients should be told an AI reads their messages.
Implementation
An approved tool covered by the clinic’s agreement, never a personal account. Test it on invented messages first, then compare its sort with nurses’ sorts and count every miss.
Limits
It can’t promise zero misses, so it must not slow down the human path for anything urgent.

Arts — a design-variation workflow

Problem
Small nonprofit clients need a dozen sizes and versions of every design, and resizing eats the designer’s time.
Solution
The designer finishes the main design; an AI tool produces layout variations from it; the designer picks, fixes and approves each one.
Ethics
Authorship: prompts alone don’t make an author, so the designer’s own changes matter. Rights: the client should know AI was used. The artists whose work trained the tool never agreed.
Implementation
Try it on the designer’s own past work and time both methods.
Limits
Variations may drift from the brand; every one still needs a designer’s eye.

Business — a customer-feedback summary

Problem
A small café gets about 200 online reviews a month, and the owner can’t read them all, so patterns get missed.
Solution
Each week the AI groups reviews by theme and counts them; the owner reads the summary plus a few full reviews from each theme and decides what to change.
Ethics
Fairness: a rare but serious complaint — an allergic reaction — could vanish inside a summary. Privacy: reviews include customers’ names.
Implementation
Paste reviews with names removed; check one month’s summary against a full read by the owner.
Limits
It counts what people wrote, not why customers who never wrote stopped coming.

Computer science — a first-pass code review

Problem
On a student team, code changes wait days for someone to review them.
Solution
An AI tool comments on every change first; a teammate still reviews and approves before anything is merged; anything touching passwords or user data always gets a full human review.
Ethics
Harms: a reviewer who trusts the AI’s silence misses a security flaw. Accountability: the approving teammate owns what gets merged.
Implementation
Run it on ten past changes with known bugs; count what it caught and what it missed, and time the reviews (the METR study is the warning).
Limits
Faster reviews only matter if the same bugs still get caught.
Caution

Keep real people out of it. Your proposal describes a kind of workplace, not a real one — “a small family clinic” is enough. Don’t name an employer, and don’t include any real person’s information, message or record.
