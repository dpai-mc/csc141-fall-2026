Three Ways to Work With AI

The difference is how much the AI does before a person looks at it.

Read this section

In M5L1 you sorted a job’s tasks into what AI could do, what it could help with, and what stays mostly unchanged. This lesson is about the middle bucket — the tasks where a person and an AI tool work together — and how to set that up so it actually helps.

Human-AI collaboration. Work designed so a person and an AI tool each do the parts they’re suited for, with the person deciding where the AI is used, checking what it produces, and owning the result.

Collaboration comes in three common shapes. They differ in how much the AI does before a person looks at it.

AI as assistant

You ask, it answers, you decide what to use. One request at a time — draft this email, summarize this article, suggest three headlines. Nothing happens unless you act on it.

AI as co-pilot

The tool works alongside you inside the task, offering suggestions as you go — completing a line of code, proposing the next sentence, flagging a number that looks off. You accept or reject each one, often in a second.

AI as agent, with oversight

The tool carries out several steps toward a goal on its own — gathering, sorting, drafting — and a person reviews the result before anything final happens. Module 6 comes back to AI that acts on its own.

Moving from assistant to agent, the AI does more between checks. That can save more time. It also means a mistake can travel further before anyone sees it — which is why the later sections of this lesson are about where to put the checks.

What the Studies Found

Four studies of real people working with AI — and the lesson in each.

Read this section

The jagged frontier

758 consultants at the Boston Consulting Group were given realistic tasks, some with AI and some without. On tasks the AI handled well, consultants using it finished about 12% more tasks, 25% faster, with about 40% higher-quality work. On a task chosen to fall just outside what the AI did well, 84% of consultants without AI got the right answer — and only 60–70% of those using AI did.

The lesson: what AI does well is uneven — a “jagged frontier” — and you can’t tell where the edge is from how confident the answer sounds.

Dell’Acqua et al., Harvard Business School working paper, September 2023.

The biggest help goes to beginners

At a company with 5,179 customer-support agents, an AI tool that suggested replies raised the number of problems solved per hour by 14% on average — and by 34% for newer, less experienced agents. For the most experienced agents, it made little difference.

The lesson: AI can spread what the best workers know to newer ones. It doesn’t replace knowing the work — the experts had already learned what the tool was suggesting.

Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025.

Feeling faster isn’t being faster

Sixteen experienced software developers worked on 246 real tasks in projects they knew well, some with AI coding tools and some without. Before starting, they expected AI to make them about 24% faster. Measured, they were 19% slower with it — time went into prompting, waiting and checking.

The lesson: measure, don’t assume. The researchers expected results to change as tools improve, and that’s the point — check it for your own work.

METR, 2025-07-10.

Skills you stop using can fade

At four clinics in Poland, 19 experienced doctors began using an AI tool that highlights growths during colonoscopies. Months later, in procedures done without the tool, their detection rate had dropped from 28.4% to 22.4%.

The lesson: leaning on a tool can wear down the skill you’d need when the tool isn’t there or is wrong — M3L2’s cognitive offloading, at work. It’s one observational study, so the cause isn’t proven.

Budzyń et al., The Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology, August 2025.

Study details accurate as of 2026-10.

Designing a Workflow

Where the person starts, where the AI helps, and where the person checks and decides.

Read this section

A workflow is just the order of steps for getting a task done. Designing a human-AI workflow means deciding, step by step, who does what. Most good ones follow the same shape:

  1. A person starts it. They decide what the task is, what a good result looks like, and what the AI needs to know — the prompting skills from Module 2.
  2. The AI does a step. Drafting, summarizing, sorting, suggesting — the parts you sorted as “AI could help with it.”
  3. A person checks. Against what? A source, a rule, a number they can verify, their own knowledge of the field. A check needs a standard, or it’s just a glance.
  4. A person decides and owns the result. Whoever signs, sends or acts on it is accountable for it, whether or not an AI drafted it.

Three questions help you decide where AI belongs in a process:

  • Which step is slow, repetitive or a blank page? That’s where AI usually helps most.
  • What would a mistake at this step cost, and who would notice it? The higher the cost, the closer the check needs to be.
  • Is this step inside or outside the frontier? If you can’t tell, test it on examples where you already know the right answer.

Write down what the AI did. At work, “which parts did a tool produce, and who checked them?” is a question a manager, a client or a court may ask. A short note — tool, step, who reviewed — is the professional version of the disclosure you wrote into your Personal AI Code in Module 3.

Try this

Think of a process you follow often — studying for a test, planning a trip, writing a lab report. Where would an AI step save the most time? Where would a mistake be hardest to catch?

When It Goes Wrong

Catching errors, knowing when to stop, and knowing who answers for it.

Read this section

Every workflow with AI in it needs answers to four questions before something goes wrong, not after.

  • How will errors be caught? Spot-checking a sample, checking every item above a certain risk, comparing against a source, having a second person look. As you learned in M4L3, people tend to go along with a system’s recommendation, especially when they’re rushed — so a check that depends on someone noticing on their own usually isn’t one.
  • When does a case go to a person? This is called escalation: the rule for when the AI step is skipped or overruled — an unusual case, an upset customer, a decision about someone’s health, money or rights.
  • Who is accountable? Follow the chain: who chose the tool, who set up the workflow, who checked the output, who signed it. Somewhere on that chain is a person who answers for the result. “The AI did it” is not an answer anyone accepts — not an employer, not a customer, not a court.
  • Does the workplace know the AI step is there? An AI tool used for work without the employer’s approval — a personal account, a free app, a browser extension — is called shadow AI. If nobody else knows the AI step exists, nobody can check it, nobody can set an escalation rule for it, and whatever was pasted into it has left the workplace.

Shadow AI is the usual case, not the rare one

In a 2025 survey of 1,000 employees and 500 security leaders, 81% of employees said they use AI tools their employer hasn’t approved. So did 88% of the security leaders — the people whose job is knowing the risks. And 45% of workers said they find ways around applications their employer blocks.

UpGuard, The State of Shadow AI, survey of 1,000 employees and 500 security leaders, 2025.

It costs something. IBM’s yearly study of data breaches found that employees’ shadow AI was involved in 43% of the breaches it examined, up from 20% the year before, and those breaches cost more on average.

IBM, Cost of a Data Breach Report 2026, 602 breaches from March 2025 to February 2026, published July 2026.

The lesson: knowing the rules doesn’t stop it — the people who know the risks best do it too. People reach for an unapproved tool when the approved path is slower or missing. A workflow holds up when the approved tool is easy to reach and good enough to use, so nobody has a reason to go around it.

Survey and breach figures accurate as of 2026-10.

Caution

A review step that never says no isn’t a review. If the person checking an AI’s work doesn’t have the time, the information or the authority to reject it, the workflow only looks supervised.

M5L2-A1 — Design a workflow, then test it

35 minutes Optional export · keep it

Take one task from your M5L1 job, map a human-AI workflow for it, run it once on an invented example, and critique what happened.

Step 1 — pick the task

Choose a task from your M5L1 sort — ideally one you marked AI could help with it. If you didn’t do M5L1, pick any task from the job you’d like to have. An item from your 10 Things list works too, if you picture doing it at work.

Step 2 — map the workflow

Write the steps in order — three to six. For each, say who does it (a person, the AI tool, or both) and how its result gets checked. Somewhere there should be a step where a person checks, and a final step where a person decides.

Step 3 — write an invented example

Make up a realistic example for the task — a sample customer email, a sample set of notes, a sample request.

Caution

Invent it. Don’t copy it. Never use a real message, document or record from a workplace, a customer, a patient or a coworker — not even with the names taken out. An invented example tests the workflow just as well.

Step 4 — run it

Open Gemini (or another tool if you prefer) in a new chat. Copy the prompt, fill in the brackets, and send it. Then do the human steps yourself: check what came back the way your workflow says.

I'm testing a workflow for this task at work: [the task]. You are the AI step in it. Here is what I need you to do: [the AI step from your workflow]. Here is the example input: [your invented example]. Do only that step, then tell me what you assumed and anything you weren't sure about.

Step 5 — critique it

Where did the workflow hold up? Where did it break — something the AI got wrong that your check would have caught, or one it wouldn’t have? What would you change?

Check Yourself

Not graded, not recorded. Four questions.

Open the four questions
1. In the consulting study, why did people using AI do worse on one task than people without it?
2. A clinic adds a step where a staff member approves every AI-drafted patient letter. Which situation makes that review step weakest?
3. In a human-AI workflow, who is accountable for a mistake in the final result?
4. A paralegal’s firm has an approved AI tool, but it’s slow, so she pastes a client’s contract into a free AI app on her phone to summarize it. What is the biggest problem?
