Six Fields, Six Real Deployments

What AI is actually doing in each — what the evidence says, and the question it raises.

Read this section

Every field is using AI differently, and the evidence about how well it works is different in each. Here is one real deployment from six fields — not the most impressive one, but one with evidence you can check. Read your own field’s card closely, and at least one from a field far from yours.

Healthcare

AI scribes and approved devices

What it is. “Ambient scribe” tools listen to a doctor’s visit and draft the clinical note. Separately, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration lists more than 1,400 AI-enabled medical devices it has authorized, most of them in radiology.

The evidence. In a randomized trial with 238 physicians, one scribe tool cut note-writing time modestly and both tools slightly improved burnout scores — but doctors reported that the drafts occasionally contained clinically significant errors, most often missing information.

The question. Who catches the error in a note the doctor didn’t write, and did the patient agree to being recorded?

NEJM AI, 2025 (UCLA Health, 2025-11-26); FDA list updated 2026-03-04 (MedTech Dive).

Education

Helping the tutor, not replacing them

What it is. Tutor CoPilot suggests responses to human tutors during live math tutoring; the tutor chooses, edits or ignores them.

The evidence. In a randomized trial with about 1,000 elementary students, those whose tutors had the tool were 4 percentage points more likely to master a topic — up to 9 points for students of lower-rated tutors — at about $20 per tutor per year.

The question. If the tool helps weaker tutors most, does it close a gap — or make it easier to hire less-trained tutors for the students who most need good ones?

Wang et al., Stanford National Student Support Accelerator, 2025.

Business

Customer service, and a reversal

What it is. In early 2024 the payments company Klarna said its AI assistant was doing the work of 700 customer-service employees.

The evidence. By May 2025 its CEO said cost had driven the decision too much and the result was “lower quality,” and the company began hiring human support staff again.

The question. Who measured “the work of 700 people” — and did that measure include whether customers’ problems were actually solved?

Klarna, 2024; Bloomberg interview reported by Computing, 2025-05-13.

Arts and design

Who is the author?

What it is. Image, music and writing generators are now part of many creative workflows, from first sketches to finished work.

The evidence. In January 2025 the U.S. Copyright Office concluded that prompts alone don’t make someone the author of what a tool generates. A person’s own expression in the work — their selection, arrangement or changes — can be protected, decided case by case.

The question. If the generated parts belong to no one, what does that mean for artists who sell their work — and for the artists whose work trained the tools?

U.S. Copyright Office, Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2, 2025-01-29.

Sciences

Predicting protein structures

What it is. AlphaFold, from Google DeepMind, predicts the 3-D shape of proteins from their sequence — work that can take a lab months or longer for a single protein.

The evidence. Its team has predicted structures for nearly all of the roughly 200 million known proteins, and two of its creators shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Its predictions were tested against structures measured in the lab, and researchers still verify the ones they rely on.

The question. When a prediction is right most of the time, how do you know which ones aren’t?

Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2024 (Science News Explores).

Law

Fabricated citations in court

What it is. Lawyers and people representing themselves use AI tools to research and draft court filings.

The evidence. As of June 2026, one tracking database listed 1,058 U.S. court cases in which a filing contained AI-generated errors such as invented cases or quotes — about 60% by people representing themselves and 40% by attorneys.

The question. The person who signs a filing is responsible for it. What would a workflow need for that signature to mean something?

UNC Law Library, June 2026, citing Charlotin’s AI hallucination cases database.

Deployments and figures accurate as of 2026-10.

Judging an AI Tool in Your Field

Four questions that work for any tool, in any field.

Read this section

Every field has AI tools built for it, and more appear every month. You don’t need to know them all. You need a way to judge the one in front of you. Ask four questions:

  1. What problem does it solve? In one sentence, for whom. If you can’t say, neither may the seller.
  2. What’s the evidence it works? This is the question that matters most, and the one most often skipped. Sort what you find:
    • An independent study — researchers who don’t sell the tool measured it, ideally against people working without it.
    • A news report — a journalist describing it, which may rest on the company’s own claims.
    • The company’s own claims — a website, a press release, a customer testimonial.
    • Nothing you can find — which is common, and worth saying.
  3. What ethical concerns does it raise? Use the three lenses from M4L5: who’s helped and who’s harmed; whose rights are involved, and whether they agreed; and whether the costs fall on the same group every time. Add Module 4’s other questions: could it be biased, and what happens to the data it takes in?
  4. When it’s wrong, who catches it? M5L2’s question. Somebody has to check the output and answer for the result.
Verify

A company describing its own tool is not evidence that the tool works. It may be true, but it’s a claim. Look for who measured it, against what, and when. “I couldn’t find any independent evidence” is a real and useful finding.

Caution

You don’t need to sign up for anything. Researching a tool means reading about it — what it does, who uses it, what’s been measured. Don’t create accounts or enter information to try a tool for this course.

Working Across Fields

Explaining your field to an AI — and borrowing from someone else’s.

Read this section

Most AI projects bring together people from different fields: someone who knows the work and someone who knows the technology. Whichever side you’re on, two skills help.

Translating your field into something an AI can use. A tool doesn’t know your field’s unwritten rules. To get useful work from it, spell them out: define the terms your field uses differently from everyone else, give an example of a good result and a bad one, and state the constraints — legal limits, safety rules, what must never happen. That’s the prompting from Module 2, applied to the knowledge only you have.

Borrowing across fields. Many of the best uses of AI were first worked out somewhere else. The tutor-support idea from education is a co-pilot for any job where newer workers handle live conversations. The scribe idea from healthcare fits any job where someone writes up a meeting. Look at the six cards above and ask: which of these would work in my field, and what would have to change?

M5L4-A1 — Two real tools in your field

30 minutes Optional export · keep it

Find two AI tools or applications used in your field, confirm they’re real, and judge each on the four questions.

Step 1 — ask for candidates

Open Gemini (or another tool if you prefer) in a new chat. Copy the prompt, fill in your M5L1 job or field, and send it.

I work in (or am studying) [your job or field]. Name four specific AI tools or applications that are actually being used in this field today. For each, give its name, who makes it, what problem it solves, and a link to a source that describes it.

Step 2 — keep two that are real

Open the sources. Keep two tools you can confirm exist and are used in your field. If one turns out not to exist, or its source doesn’t say what the tool claimed, note it — that’s a finding — and pick another.

Step 3 — judge each one

Answer the four questions for both. For the evidence question, look beyond the company’s own website: search for the tool’s name with words like “study,” “trial” or “evaluation.”

Step 4 — one to borrow

Optional, for Monday: one AI application from a different field that you’d borrow for yours.
