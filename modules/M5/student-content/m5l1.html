Two Stories About AI and Work

You’ve heard both. The useful question is which one fits a particular job.

Read this section

Module 4 asked what goes wrong when AI makes decisions about people. Module 5 asks a question closer to home: what does AI mean for the work you do now, or the work you’re training for?

There are two stories you’ll hear about that, and both are told with confidence.

The displacement story

AI can now write, summarize, translate, answer customers and draft code. Work that people are paid to do will be done by software instead, and the people who did it will lose those jobs. The faster the tools improve, the faster it happens.

The transformation story

AI changes what jobs consist of, the way computers and the internet did. Some tasks disappear, others get faster, and new kinds of work appear. Most people keep their jobs but do them differently — and the people who learn the tools do best.

History gives some support to each. Machines have wiped out whole occupations — there are very few switchboard operators or typesetters left. But they’ve also changed jobs without ending them. When cash machines (ATMs) spread through the 1980s and 1990s, the number of bank tellers in the U.S. didn’t fall the way people expected. Each branch needed fewer tellers, so banks opened more branches, and tellers spent less time counting cash and more time with customers (Bessen, Toil and Technology, IMF, 2015).

So neither story is simply right. The rest of this lesson gives you a way to decide which one fits a particular job — including the one you want — and a look at what the evidence actually shows so far.

Jobs Are Made of Tasks

A job is rarely automated whole. Tasks are.

Read this section

“Will AI replace nurses?” is almost impossible to answer, because a nurse does dozens of different things in a shift. “Could AI draft a nurse’s shift-change notes?” is a question you can actually think about. That shift — from the job to the tasks inside it — is the most useful habit in this module.

Task-level analysis (of jobs). Looking at a job as the separate tasks it’s made of, rather than as one thing, and asking about each task whether AI could do it, help with it, or leave it mostly unchanged. A job is rarely automated whole; tasks are.

For each task, there are three honest answers:

  • AI could do it. The tool does the task in the person’s place — sorting routine requests, filling in a standard form, transcribing a recording. That’s automation.
  • AI could help with it. The person still does the task and is still responsible for it, but faster or better with the tool — a first draft to edit, a summary to check, a list of options to choose from.
  • Mostly unchanged. The task depends on being physically there, on a relationship, or on judgment the tool can’t supply — comforting a patient, inspecting a weld, deciding what to do when the rules don’t cover the case.

AI augmentation. Using AI to help a person do a task better or faster while the person keeps doing it and stays responsible for it — as opposed to automation, where the AI does the task in the person’s place.

Then ask one more question about every task, whichever bucket it’s in: does it need human judgment? Judgment is what’s needed when the right answer depends on context the tool doesn’t have, when a mistake would hurt someone, or when someone has to be accountable for the result. A task can be one AI could help with and still need a person to make the final call — most of the important ones are.

Note

The mix is what matters. A job where most tasks are “AI could do it” is a job likely to shrink. A job where most are “AI could help” is a job likely to change. Most jobs have some of each, which is why both stories in the last section are partly true.

What the Evidence Shows So Far

Real data, real disagreement — and why both deserve your attention.

Read this section

Headlines about AI and jobs tend to pick a story first and find a number to fit it. Researchers have been measuring what has actually happened since late 2022, when ChatGPT made generative AI widely available. Here’s where the evidence stood as of October 2026.

A signal among young workers

Using payroll records from millions of U.S. workers, Stanford researchers found that employment for 22- to 25-year-olds in the jobs most exposed to AI fell about 13% relative to other workers after late 2022. Older workers in the same jobs held steady or grew. The drop was concentrated in jobs where AI tends to do the task rather than help with it.

Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, Canaries in the Coal Mine?, Stanford Digital Economy Lab, August 2025.

No sign of a broad shake-up

Looking across the whole economy, Yale’s Budget Lab found no clear disruption of the labor market since ChatGPT’s release. Researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago found that employment in the most AI-exposed occupations actually grew about 4% from 2019 to 2024.

Yale Budget Lab, reported in Fortune, 2026-02-02; Chicago Fed researchers, reported 2026-07-24.

Why the data is hard to read

Hiring for young workers in those same jobs started slowing in 2022, when interest rates rose — before ChatGPT existed. A 2026 follow-up that controlled for this found the entry-level drop wasn’t clear until 2024. And some companies blame AI for layoffs that had other causes — sometimes called “AI washing.” Of U.S. job cuts announced in the first 11 months of 2025, about 4.5% were attributed to AI.

Mahoney, McEntarfer & Wahal, Stanford SIEPR policy brief, July 2026; Challenger, Gray & Christmas data via Oxford Economics, reported in Fortune, 2026-02-02.

Projections are not data

The World Economic Forum surveyed more than 1,000 employers, who expect about 170 million jobs to be created and 92 million displaced worldwide by 2030 — changes driven by AI and by other trends together. That’s a forecast of what employers expect, not a measurement of what happened.

World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs Report 2025, 2025-01-08.

What to take from it. So far, the clearest effect is not mass job loss. It’s fewer entry-level openings in a handful of AI-exposed jobs — the jobs where people traditionally learned the work. Experience seems to protect people, which says something about which skills are worth building. And researchers still disagree about how much of that is AI.

Verify

“Exposed” doesn’t mean “doomed.” Studies that rank jobs by how much AI overlaps with their tasks say so themselves: a high score means the work is likely to change, not that the job will disappear (Microsoft Research, Working with AI, July 2025). When you see a list of “jobs AI will replace,” ask what was measured — overlap, a forecast, or actual employment.

Figures accurate as of 2026-10. This is fast-moving research; newer studies may change the picture.

Applying It to Your Field

The job you want, one task at a time.

Read this section

Everything in this module is built around one job: one you have, one you’re training for, or one you’d like. You’ll choose it in this lesson’s activity, and it carries through every lesson until the mini-project at the end of Module 5 — where you propose an AI solution to a real problem in that field.

To analyze a job, do it in this order:

  1. List the tasks. Not the job title — the things a person actually spends their week doing. An AI tool is good at drafting this list, and that part is fine to hand off.
  2. Sort them yourself. Could AI do it, help with it, or leave it mostly unchanged — and does it need human judgment? This is where your knowledge of the field matters, and where you shouldn’t let a tool decide for you.
  3. Then compare. Ask the tool to sort the same list and look at where you disagree. A disagreement is worth more than an agreement: either you know something about the work the tool doesn’t, or it’s seeing something you missed.
Try this

Think about a job you’ve actually done — even a summer job. Which part of it would you most have liked a tool to help with? Which part could no tool have done? You already know more about task-level analysis than you think.

M5L1-A1 — Sort a job into its tasks

30 minutes Optional export · keep it

Choose one job, get its tasks, sort every task yourself, then compare your sort with an AI tool’s.

Step 1 — choose your job

The job you’re training for, one you have now, or one you’d like. Not sure what field you’re headed for? A field you’re considering, or a job you’ve held, works just as well. You’ll use this same job for the rest of Module 5, so pick one you’re curious about.

Caution

Describe the job, not your workplace. “Front-desk receptionist at a dental office” is all the tool needs. Don’t name an employer, a coworker or a customer, and don’t paste anything from a real workplace — for the rest of this module, too.

Step 2 — get the task list

Open Gemini (or another tool if you prefer) in a new chat. Copy the prompt, replace the bracket with your job, and send it.

I'm studying the job of [the job]. List the 8 most important tasks a person in this job does in a typical week. One short line per task. Don't say anything about AI yet.

Copy the tasks into the boxes below. If a task on the list is wrong for the job as you know it, fix it or swap it — you know the work better than the tool does.

Step 3 — sort every task yourself

For each task, choose AI could do it, AI could help with it or Mostly unchanged; tick whether it needs human judgment; and write your reason in a sentence. Leave the last drop-down for Step 4.

Step 4 — then ask the tool

Only after you’ve sorted all eight, send this in the same chat and record the tool’s answer for each task in its drop-down.

For each task on your list, say whether AI could do it, could help with it, or would leave it mostly unchanged, with a one-sentence reason. Then name the tasks that most need human judgment.

Step 5 — look at where you disagree

Pick the disagreement that matters most. Who’s right, and what does one of you know that the other doesn’t? If you agreed on every task, say which sort you’re least sure of, and why.

Not graded, not recorded. Three questions.
