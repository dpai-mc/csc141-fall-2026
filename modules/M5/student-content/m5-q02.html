What You’ll Do

A real problem in your field, and a careful proposal for how AI could help.

Read this section

Propose an AI solution to a real problem in your field — designed carefully enough that someone in that field could judge whether it’s worth trying. It’s Module 5’s graded artifact, and it closes with your Module 5 reflection. The five steps and four worked examples are in M5L5; this page is where you do it.

The five parts

  • Part 1 — the problem: a specific problem in your field, who has it, and how you’d know it was solved.
  • Part 2 — the solution: what the AI does, why AI fits, and the workflow that connects it to the people doing the work.
  • Part 3 — the ethics: your solution judged through at least one lens, with fairness, privacy, transparency and accountability.
  • Part 4 — the implementation: the data, tools, people and small pilot you’d need, and how you’d measure it.
  • Part 5 — the limits: what it can’t do, what could go wrong, and how you used AI on this project.

Format & length

  • Typed into this page. About one or two paragraphs per part — two to three pages exported.
  • Module reflection: 3–5 sentences answering all three prompts.
Note

Using AI on this project. You can use Gemini or another tool to brainstorm, to poke holes in your idea, or to improve your wording. The proposal must be your own synthesis — your problem, your judgment, your field’s knowledge — and Part 5 asks you to say how you used AI.

Caution

This proposal goes to your instructor. Describe a kind of workplace, not a real one — “a small family clinic” is enough. Don’t name an employer, and don’t include any real person’s information, message or record. You can earn full credit without either.

Work block 2 — the ethical analysis

25 minutes Earns: Ethical analysis

Judge your own solution — through at least one lens, and on four concerns.

Note

The three lenses from M4L5:

  • Harms and benefits — Who is helped, who is harmed, and how badly? Does the good outweigh the harm for everyone affected — not just for the person using the tool?
  • Rights — Does this respect each person’s dignity and their right to decide about their own information? Did the people affected agree, and can they find out what happened and challenge it?
  • Fairness — Are people who are alike in the ways that matter treated alike? Do the costs fall on everyone, or on the same group every time?

Your judgment can be acceptable as designed, acceptable only with conditions or not acceptable as designed — judging your own idea harshly is allowed.

Work block 3 — the implementation sketch

20 minutes Earns: Implementation sketch

Say what you’d need to try this, and how you’d know whether it worked.

Name only tools you could actually reach — your college tools always count, and so do tools you researched in M5L4 — and say how you’d get access. At a real workplace, “could reach” also means “would be approved”: a tool the workplace hasn’t approved is shadow AI (M5L2), and your workflow’s checks can’t cover it. Don’t describe what a named product can do; describe what your solution needs.

Work block 4 — limits, risks and your AI-use note

15 minutes Earns: Reflection on limits

Be honest about what your proposal can’t do — and about how you used AI to make it.

Closing — your Module 5 reflection

20 minutes Portfolio

Looking back across this module — your job as tasks, the evidence about AI and work, the workflow you tested, the role you checked, the tools you judged and the proposal you just wrote — answer the following in 3–5 sentences:

  1. Since your M5L1 task sort, has your view of AI in your field moved — toward displacement, toward transformation, or neither? What moved it?
  2. Which piece of evidence from this module — a study, a case, or your own test — will you remember the next time someone makes a confident claim about AI and jobs?
  3. Name one skill you’ll build because of this module, and one thing about AI in your field that you think people outside it misunderstand.
