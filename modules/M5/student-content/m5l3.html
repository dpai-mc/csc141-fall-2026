New Roles Created by AI

Where the new jobs are actually showing up.

Read this section

M5L1 was about how AI changes existing jobs. AI also creates new ones. Each January, LinkedIn ranks the job titles growing fastest on its site. Four of its top five for the U.S. in 2026 were tied to AI:

  1. AI engineers — building AI into products and systems.
  2. AI consultants and strategists — helping organizations decide where AI fits and how to adopt it.
  3. New home sales specialists (not AI-related).
  4. Data annotators — labeling and reviewing the data AI systems learn from.
  5. AI and machine-learning researchers.

LinkedIn, Jobs on the Rise 2026, 2026-01-07 — based on changes in job titles on LinkedIn profiles, 2023–2025.

Those titles are the visible part. Most new AI work falls into four broad families, and only the first requires deep technical training:

Building it

Engineering and research roles that create and run AI systems — including the people who keep models working reliably once they’re in use, sometimes called ML operations.

Fitting it to the work

Roles that sit between a field and the technology: consultants, AI product managers, and specialists inside a profession — the nurse who leads a hospital’s AI pilot, the paralegal who sets up a firm’s research tools.

Teaching it

Labeling data, writing examples and rating AI answers so models improve — work often called AI training or data annotation. Much of it is contract work, and some of it asks for people who already know a field well enough to judge answers in it.

Checking it

Roles that test AI for bias, safety and compliance, and that write the rules organizations follow — the work you practiced in your Module 4 bias audit.

Rankings accurate as of 2026-10.

The Prompt Engineer Story

A job title that rose and faded — and the skill that stayed.

Read this section

In 2023, “prompt engineer” was called the hottest new job in tech — a role for people who knew how to talk to AI, with reported salaries up to $200,000 and no coding required. Job searches for it peaked in April 2023 and have fallen since.

What happened? The tools got better at understanding ordinary requests and asking clarifying questions, and writing good prompts turned into something many workers are expected to do. As an economist at the job site Indeed put it, prompt engineering is still a good skill to have, “but it’s not an entire title.”

Fortune, 2025-05-07, citing Indeed Hiring Lab.

That’s the pattern to remember. A skill that is new and scarce can look like a job for a while. Once many people have it, it becomes part of other jobs. The prompting you practiced in Module 2 didn’t lose its value — it became part of the baseline.

Verify

Check any “hot new AI job” before you plan around it. Search for real postings with that title, and notice the date on anything you read. A role that was everywhere in the news two years ago may barely exist as a title now.

Skills That Last

What stays valuable when tools keep changing.

Read this section

The research in M5L1 and M5L2 points the same way. Young workers in AI-exposed jobs saw fewer openings while experienced workers in the same jobs held steady. Expert support agents gained little from AI because they already knew what it was suggesting. Knowing your field well is the skill AI makes more valuable, not less — it’s what lets you tell a good AI answer from a confident wrong one.

Skills that hold their value

  • Domain expertise — deep knowledge of your field’s work, rules and judgment calls.
  • Critical thinking — checking claims, spotting what’s missing, the M3L1 habit.
  • Working with people — listening, explaining, persuading, caring for someone.
  • Creative judgment — deciding what’s worth making and when it’s good.

Skills to build now

  • AI literacy — what this course is for: using the tools well and knowing their limits.
  • Data fluency — reading a chart, a table or a statistic and knowing what it does and doesn’t show.
  • Adaptability — learning a new tool or process without starting from zero.
  • Working across fields — explaining your field to people outside it, and borrowing ideas from theirs.

Employers say much the same. Asked which skills will grow most in importance by 2030, more than 1,000 employers surveyed by the World Economic Forum put AI and big data first, followed by networks and cybersecurity, technological literacy, creative thinking, and resilience, flexibility and agility.

World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs Report 2025, 2025-01-08.

Building Your Path

Practical moves that work in any field.

Read this section
  • Keep learning on a schedule, not in a panic. Tools change monthly. Pick one way to keep up — a newsletter in your field, a professional group, a short course a year — and stick with it.
  • Show what you can do with AI, not just that you’ve used it. Your course portfolio is already a start: a record of AI-assisted work with your judgment and checking visible in it. That’s more convincing to an employer than listing tools on a résumé.
  • Talk to people doing the work. An informational interview is a short conversation — 20 minutes, often by phone — with someone in a job you’re curious about, to ask how it’s really changing. People say yes more often than you’d expect.
  • Use public career data. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics’ Occupational Outlook Handbook and O*NET OnLine describe what thousands of jobs involve, what they pay and how fast they’re growing — free, and not written by anyone selling a tool.
Try this

Look up your M5L1 job in O*NET. It lists the tasks of each occupation. Compare its list with the one your AI tool gave you in M5L1 — what did the tool leave out?

M5L3-A1 — One new role, verified, and your skills against it

25 minutes Optional export · keep it

Find one emerging role where AI meets your field, check that it’s real, and take stock of what you’d need for it.

Step 1 — ask for a role

Open Gemini (or another tool if you prefer) in a new chat. Copy the prompt, fill in your M5L1 job or field, and send it.

I'm preparing for a career as [your job or field]. Name one emerging job role that combines AI with this field. Give its usual job title, three things a person in that role does, and three skills it requires.

Step 2 — check that it’s real

Search the web yourself — not by asking the tool — for one real job posting or one industry article that describes this role. Record where you found it and its date. If the title doesn’t turn up, try a close variation; if nothing matches, that’s a finding, and you should say so.

Step 3 — take stock of your skills

Using the posting or article (not just the tool’s list), choose three skills the role asks for. For each, say honestly whether you have it, partly have it, or don’t have it yet. Then name one step you could take toward one gap.
