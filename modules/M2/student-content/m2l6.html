What a Casebook Is

A worked record — not a summary of what you learned, the actual material.

Read this section

A casebook is a worked record. Not a summary of what you learned — the actual material, kept in enough detail that someone else could see what happened, including you in three months.

The form is borrowed. Lawyers keep casebooks; so do engineers, clinicians, and anyone whose judgement improves by accumulating cases rather than by reading one more explanation. What makes it a casebook rather than a folder is that each entry carries the thing you did, the thing you got, and what you made of it. Drop any one of the three and it stops being useful: the prompt without the output is a guess, the output without the prompt is unrepeatable, and both without your reading of them is a pile of text you will never open again.

For prompt engineering the payoff is unusually direct, because the whole subject is a skill you can only see from the outside. You cannot tell whether a prompt was good by looking at the prompt. You find out by looking at what came back — and you learn the general lesson by looking at several of those next to each other, which is the one thing a single conversation can never show you.

So: one task, run more than once, written down, and read. That is the entire idea. Everything else in this lesson is about doing each of those four things well enough that the exercise pays.

Choosing a Task Worth Three Runs

Made in the first five minutes, before any tool is open.

Read this section

This is the decision that determines whether the exercise teaches you anything, and it is made in the first five minutes, before any tool is open.

What makes a task work

  • It is real. Something you actually want done — from your own field, your own coursework, or your “10 Things” list. A made-up task produces made-up judgement, because you have no standard to hold the answers to.
  • You know enough to judge the answer. Not everything about it — enough to tell a good answer from a plausible-sounding one. This is the criterion people skip, and it is the one that decides whether your critiques are worth reading.
  • It has requirements you can name. An audience, a length, a format, a thing to leave out. Requirements are what make differences visible: if you asked for under 200 words, you can see who ignored you.
  • It is small enough to finish. One deliverable, not a project.

What makes a task useless

  • It is too easy to get wrong in an interesting way. “Write a poem about cats.” All the answers will be fine, you will have nothing to say about any of them, and you will conclude that the tools are much the same — which is a conclusion about your task, not about anything else.
  • It is outside what you can evaluate. If you cannot tell whether the answer is any good, you are grading fluency, which every one of these tools has in quantity.
  • It is enormous. A whole essay, a full study plan, a complete lesson. You will get several long answers and read none of them properly.

Use the same prompt every time. The point of running a task more than once is to hold something constant so that what varies is visible. Improving the prompt between runs is a good instinct, and it is a different exercise — one you already did. Here, the prompt is the control.

Try this

Say out loud what would make one answer better than another. Before you open anything, try to name it — not “better written,” but something you could point at afterwards. If you cannot name it for the task you picked, pick a different one: you have just found out that you would be grading these on how they sound. The studio page opens with the exact test.

Judging an Output: Five Criteria

A critique names what in the output produced the judgement.

Read this section

A critique is not a rating. A critique names what in the output produced the judgement — the sentence, the omission, the wrong register — and a good one is often shorter than a paragraph.

These are the five this course uses. You will not use all five on every output; two or three, chosen because they are what this task actually needs, beats five applied dutifully.

Accuracy. Is it right about the things you already know enough to check? You brought this task because you know something about it — so check the parts you can: names, dates, steps, claims about your own field. You are not expected to go and research the parts you can’t check. If two answers disagree and you cannot tell which is right, that is not a dead end — write that down. It is one of the more useful things a casebook can record.

Completeness. Did it do the whole task, or the easy two-thirds of it? Look specifically for the requirement you put last, and for anything you asked it not to do. Instructions get dropped from the end and from the negative.

Tone and appropriateness. Is it pitched at the audience you named? Read one paragraph as if you were the person it is for. Wrong register is the failure that survives every other check, because the text is perfectly good — for somebody else.

Creativity. Did it bring anything you would not have thought of? This is the criterion that separates useful from merely correct, and it is worth being honest in both directions: a task that did not call for an original idea should not be marked down for not having one.

Usefulness. The one that matters most and gets asked least: would you actually use this? Not “is it impressive,” not “is it well written.” Would this go in the thing you were making, with or without edits — and if with, how many?

Accuracy

The question it asks: is it right about the things you can already check?

Where it usually fails: a confident sentence about your own field that you know is not quite right.

Judge the output you got, not the one you could have got with a better prompt. You already know how to improve a prompt; that is not what is being measured here. Read what came back against what you asked for.

Note

Two or three criteria, chosen because the task needs them, beats all five applied out of duty. A critique that says something specific about completeness and nothing about creativity is a better critique than one that says something vague about each.

What Makes a Comparison Worth Reading

The part that turns several entries into one thing.

Read this section

The last part of a casebook is the part that turns several entries into one thing: a short synthesis, written after you have read all of them.

Caution

One run is not evidence about a tool. You will have one output from each, on one task, from one prompt, on one day — and there is randomness in all of it. Comparing the outputs in front of you is the exercise. Ranking the products is a different claim, and it is one this evidence cannot carry.

Compare the outputs, not the products. This is the discipline the whole exercise rests on, and it is the opposite of what feels natural. You ran one task, once, through each tool, with one prompt, on one day. That is not enough to conclude anything about any of the tools, and any sentence of the form “X is better than Y” is a claim your evidence does not support. It is also the kind of claim that stops being true quietly, months before anyone notices.

What your evidence does support is much more interesting, because it is about you and your task:

  • What they all did the same. If every answer missed the same requirement, the requirement was buried, or ambiguous, or last. That is a finding about your prompt, and it is the single most valuable thing this exercise produces.
  • Where they diverged, and on what. Not which was best — on what dimension they differed. Length? Register? How much they assumed about the reader? That tells you which parts of your request were doing work and which were being interpreted.
  • What surprised you. Including the boring surprise: that they were all much the same. If that is what happened, it is worth asking whether the task was one where the answer was overdetermined — which is a real result and a good one to know.
  • What you would change about the prompt, now that you have seen several attempts at answering it. One sentence.

The reader of your synthesis is you, later. Write the thing you would want to find if you came back to this task in six months having forgotten everything except that you once looked into it.

Check Yourself

Not graded, not recorded. Three questions.

Open the three questions
1. You run the same task through several tools and every one of them ignores the length limit you set. What is the most useful thing to write in your synthesis?
2. Two of your outputs state different things about your topic and you do not know which is right. What belongs in the casebook?
3. Which of these is the best reason to reject a task before you start?
