A Third Way to Get a Prompt

You have done this two ways already. Here is the one most people never try.

Read this section

In M2L3 you wrote a prompt yourself, from five parts, and compared four versions of it. In M2L4 you sent one and then kept the conversation going until the answer was what you wanted.

There is a third route: hand the job of writing the prompt to the tool.

It sounds circular — you need a good prompt to get a good prompt — and it mostly isn’t, for one reason. Writing a prompt asks you to state things you often haven’t decided yet: who the audience is, how long it should be, what to leave out. A tool asked to draft your prompt will ask you those questions, or answer them for you. Either way you find out what you hadn’t decided.

Here is the whole week on one line:

Note

M2L3: you write the prompt. M2L4: you refine the answer. This lesson: the model drafts the prompt, and you edit it.

What is being generated is different in each case. That is the entire distinction, and it is worth holding on to, because the three are easy to blur together and they are genuinely different skills.

Giving It the Job of Prompt Engineer

The role element from M2L3, pointed somewhere unusual.

Read this section

Instead of asking the tool to be an advisor, a technical writer or a tutor, you ask it to be the person who writes the request — and you make the request itself the thing it produces.

The weak version is the obvious one:

Write me a good prompt for making a study guide.

You will get something. It will be generic, because you were. Everything that would have made the prompt yours — the course, the exam, how much time you have, what you already understand — is missing, so the draft is a prompt anyone could have asked for.

The version worth learning does two things differently: it assigns the job explicitly, and it makes the tool ask before it writes.

Role: You are a prompt engineer. Your job is to write a prompt for me
to use with an AI tool - not to do the task itself.
Task: I want a prompt that will produce a study guide for me.
Before you write anything, ask me the questions you need answered to
write a good one. Ask them all at once, numbered, and stop there.
Format: Once I answer, give me the finished prompt in a plain block I
can copy, with nothing else around it.

Two lines in there are doing the work. “Not to do the task itself” stops it from simply producing a study guide, which is what it will otherwise do. “Stop there” stops it from asking questions and then immediately answering them on your behalf — which it will also otherwise do, and which defeats the whole point.

What comes back is a list of questions about your own task. That list is the useful part even if you throw the prompt away. It is a fast way to discover that you hadn’t decided what the study guide is for.

Try this

Read the questions before you answer them. When it comes back with a numbered list of questions about your task, read the whole list first. The questions you can’t answer straight away are the ones worth thinking about — they are the parts of the job you hadn’t pinned down yet, and they would have been guessed for you.

Reading It Like an Editor

The part that matters, and what M2L3’s five parts are for.

Read this section

The prompt that comes back is a draft of your request. It is not a discovery of what you want. Nobody on the other end of the conversation knows what you want — it has your answers to a handful of questions and it is filling in the rest with whatever is most typical. Your job is to read it like an editor reads a draft: as something to strike, correct and complete before it ships. You have the vocabulary for that from M2L3, and this is what it is for.

Three things to look for, every time:

Specifics you never supplied

What it looks like: the draft has quietly settled on a reading level, an audience, a length or a tone that you never mentioned.

What to do about it: every one of those is a requirement, and requirements belong to you — strike the ones you didn’t choose, or choose them on purpose.

Padding that does nothing

What it looks like: “be thorough and accurate,” “use a professional tone,” “make it engaging” — lines that read like instructions and narrow nothing.

What to do about it: delete any line that would not change the result if it weren’t there. Nothing was ever going to aim for shallow and unprofessional.

Whatever you actually care about, missing

What it looks like: an absence — don’t recommend a decision, no bullet points, assume no statistics background, under 200 words is exactly the line that isn’t there.

What to do about it: read the draft against the five parts and find the empty slot. This is the important one, and the easiest to miss.

The habit to take from this is small and it is the point of the lesson: a prompt you didn’t write is a prompt you haven’t decided on yet. Signing off on it is the work.

Caution

A generated prompt is a draft of your request, not a discovery of what you want. It has your answers to a few questions and fills in the rest with whatever is most typical. Every specific in it that you did not choose is a decision made for you — and the decisions in a prompt are the part that makes the result yours.

When This Is Worth It

Not always — and knowing when is part of the skill.

Read this section

It earns its keep on a blank page — when you know roughly what you want and have no idea how to ask, which is the moment most people give up and type six words. It earns it again when you can’t yet articulate the task, because the questions it asks you are more useful than the prompt it writes. And it is the fastest way to get a first draft of a reusable template, of the kind M2L4 had you build out of six rounds of conversation.

It is not worth it when you can already write the prompt. Then this is two extra steps to arrive somewhere you could have gone directly. The technique is a way out of a blank page, not a replacement for knowing what you want.

M2L4a-A1 — Commission a prompt, then edit it

15 minutes Optional export

Have a tool draft a prompt for you, then edit it into one you would actually send.

Step 1 — reuse a task you have already thought about

Take the task you took through a conversation in M2L4-A1. You already have a consolidated prompt for it, which is what makes this a fair comparison.

If you would rather use a different real task — from your own field, or from your “10 Things” list — that works too. Your 10-Things list stays yours; nothing here is turned in.

Step 2 — hand over the job

Open Gemini (or another tool if you prefer) and start a new conversation. Send the prompt-engineer prompt from Giving It the Job of Prompt Engineer above, with your own task in place of the study guide. Answer its questions. Keep the finished prompt it gives you, exactly as it came back.

Step 3 — edit it

Read the draft against M2L3’s five parts and against the three checks above. Find at least one specific it settled on that you never supplied, and at least one thing you care about that it left out. Then rewrite it into the prompt you would actually send.

Step 4 — one run, one verdict

Run your edited version once, in a fresh conversation. Then answer in one or two sentences: did it beat the consolidated prompt you wrote yourself in M2L4-A1 — and, either way, what does that tell you about which parts of a prompt actually carry the weight?

Note

A tie is a real result. If the prompt you wrote yourself did just as well, you have learned something worth more than a better study guide: you can already do this part. The technique is for the tasks where you can’t.

Check Yourself

Not graded, not recorded. Three questions.
