The Part You Actually Control

One thing in this arrangement is yours to change.

Read this section

You cannot change how the model was trained. You cannot see its parameters. You cannot make it care about your assignment.

What you can change is the request.

That sounds small, and it is the whole job. The same model, asked two different ways, produces work of genuinely different quality — not because one version unlocked a hidden capability, but because a vague request is genuinely harder to answer well than a clear one. Ask a competent colleague to “write something about the budget” and you will get something. Ask them to “write a one-page summary of last quarter’s overspending for the department chair, in plain language, ending with two recommendations,” and you will get something you can use. Nothing about the colleague changed.

Prompt engineering is the name for taking that seriously: the practice of designing inputs (prompts) to guide AI systems toward desired outputs. It includes techniques for specificity, context-setting, iterative refinement, and domain adaptation.

This lesson covers the first two — how to build one good request. The next lesson covers refinement: what to do with the answer once it comes back.

Note

Two lessons, one skill. Prompt engineering covers both building a request and refining what comes back. This lesson is the building half — how to write one good prompt before you send it. M2L4, later this week, is the refining half: what to do with the answer once you have it. The two activities are deliberately different shapes, so do this one as written rather than getting a head start.

Five Parts of a Prompt

Not a checklist. A diagnostic.

Read this section

Most well-built prompts contain some combination of five things. You will not need all five every time, and there is no prize for using all five. But when a prompt isn’t working, the fastest way to find out why is to ask which of these is missing.

  • Role. Who you want the response written as. “You are a volunteer coordinator.” “Answer as a patient tutor.”
  • Task. The verb. What you actually want done: write, summarize, compare, list, explain, rewrite, critique. This is the only one of the five that is never optional.
  • Context. What the model would need to know that it has no way to know. Who the audience is, what happened already, what you have tried, what the constraints of your situation are. This is the part people skip, and it is usually the part that was missing.
  • Constraints. The boundaries. Length, reading level, what to avoid, what must be included, what to assume the reader already knows.
  • Format. The shape of the answer. Paragraphs, a numbered list, a table, an email, bullet points with headings.
Role
“You are the volunteer coordinator at a small-town animal shelter.”
Task
“Write a short talk encouraging people to sign up.”
Context
“The audience is a monthly community meeting of about forty people, most of them retirees who already donate money but have never volunteered their time. We need weekday morning help most, and our last recruitment push brought in two people.”
Constraints
“Keep it under 300 words and under three minutes spoken. Name one concrete two-hour weekday task someone could do. Do not guilt the audience about donating money instead of time, and do not use the word ‘passionate.’”
Format
“Open with a specific story rather than a statistic. End with the single next step someone should take this week.”
Caution

A role is a steer, not a qualification. Writing “you are a nurse” changes the vocabulary, framing and assumptions of the answer. It does not give the answer a nurse’s judgment, and it is not a reason to trust it further. Use roles to shape how something is written, never as evidence about whether it is right.

Specific Beats Vague

One request, built up in four steps.

Read this section

Here is one request built up in four steps. Read what changes at each rung, and — more importantly — read what doesn’t change: it is the same topic and the same underlying want the whole way down.

  1. Rung 1 — the vague version

    Write about volunteering.

    Something will come back. It will be true, general, and useless to anybody in particular, because nothing in the request said who it was for or what it was supposed to do.

  2. Rung 2 — add the task and get specific about it

    Write a short talk encouraging people to volunteer at a small-town animal shelter.

    Now there is a verb and a subject. This is already a large improvement, and it is where most people stop.

  3. Rung 3 — add a role and context

    You are the volunteer coordinator at a small-town animal shelter. Write a short
talk encouraging people to sign up. The audience is a monthly community meeting
of about forty people, most of them retirees who already donate money but have
never volunteered their time. We need weekday morning help most, and our last
recruitment push brought in two people.

    The audience, the history and the actual need are things the model had no way to know and could not have guessed. Notice that the last recruitment push bringing in two people is the kind of detail that would never occur to you to include, and is exactly what makes the talk sound like it came from someone who works there.

  4. Rung 4 — add constraints and format

    You are the volunteer coordinator at a small-town animal shelter. Write a short
talk encouraging people to sign up. The audience is a monthly community meeting
of about forty people, most of them retirees who already donate money but have
never volunteered their time. We need weekday morning help most, and our last
recruitment push brought in two people.

Keep it under 300 words and under three minutes spoken. Open with a specific
story rather than a statistic. Name one concrete two-hour weekday task someone
could do. Do not guilt the audience about donating money instead of time, and do
not use the word "passionate." End with the single next step someone should take
this week.

Rung 4 is not “better writing” than rung 1. It is a different request — one that could only be answered one way, where rung 1 could have been answered a thousand ways and the model picked one.

That is the actual mechanism, and it is worth holding onto: specificity does not improve the answer, it narrows the range of possible answers. You are not making the model smarter. You are removing the room it had to guess wrong in.

Showing Instead of Telling

When the pattern is easier to demonstrate than to describe.

Read this section

Sometimes the pattern you want is easier to demonstrate than to describe. Rather than explaining the format in words, you give two or three worked examples inside the prompt itself and let the last one trail off.

Few-shot prompting is providing the model examples of desired input/output pairs inside the prompt.

Turn each note into a single calendar entry in the form "Day - Time - What."

Note: dentist thursday 2pm
Entry: Thursday - 2:00 PM - Dentist appointment

Note: coffee w/ sam, tues morning sometime around 10
Entry: Tuesday - 10:00 AM - Coffee with Sam

Note: group project meeting weds night 7
Entry:

Nothing in that prompt explains that days are capitalized, that times get a colon and an AM/PM, that “w/” becomes “with,” or that a fragment becomes a proper phrase. The examples carry all of it. Writing those rules out in prose would take a paragraph and would still miss something.

Two examples is usually enough to establish a pattern. Ten is not ten times better — it mostly makes the prompt long.

Few-shot is worth reaching for when the shape of the answer matters more than its content: consistent formatting, a house style, a classification with categories you have defined yourself, or anything you are about to do forty times.

Three Ways a Prompt Goes Wrong

Failures in the request, not in the reply.

Read this section

These are failures in the request, not in the reply. All three are yours to fix.

Ambiguity

What it is: the request has more than one reasonable reading.

Clean up my notes.

What you get instead: fix the typos? Reorganize into sections? Cut it by half? Rewrite it in full sentences? Every one of those is a defensible reading of “clean up,” and you will get exactly one of them.

Conflicting instructions

What it is: the request asks for two things that cannot both happen.

Write a thorough, comprehensive overview of the causes of the 2008 financial
crisis, covering all major perspectives, in exactly two sentences.

What you get instead: “comprehensive,” “all major perspectives” and “exactly two sentences” cannot be satisfied at once. Something has to give, and you did not get to choose which.

Over-constraining

What it is: so many requirements that they crowd out the thing you wanted.

Write a 250-word paragraph - exactly 250 words - about renewable energy that
includes the terms "grid," "baseload," "intermittency," "capacity factor," and
"levelized cost," avoids the words "green," "clean," "sustainable," "future,"
and "revolution," uses no sentence longer than fifteen words, opens with a
question, closes with a statistic, and maintains a formal but approachable tone
throughout.

What you get instead: each requirement is reasonable. Together they turn the task into a checklist to be satisfied, and what comes back reads like one. Constraints are load-bearing up to a point and then they become the whole building.

What all three have in common is the part worth remembering. In every case the model produces something. It does not stop, ask which reading you meant, or tell you that two of your instructions contradicted each other. It resolves the problem silently and hands you a confident-looking answer built on a guess you never saw it make.

So when an answer is disappointing, the first question is not what’s wrong with this tool. It is what did my prompt leave open?

Caution

It will not tell you that your prompt was broken. An ambiguous request, a self-contradicting one and an over-stuffed one all produce a fluent, confident answer. There is no error message and no “did you mean.” When a result disappoints you, re-read what you asked before you blame what answered.

M2L3-A1 — Build the ladder yourself

20 minutes Optional export

Build the same request four times, from vague to specific, and find out which rung was worth climbing.

Step 1 — start here

Your version 1 is this prompt, exactly as written:

Write about AI.

If you would rather work in your own field, you may substitute an equally vague prompt of your own — one short sentence, no role, no context, no constraints, no format. “Write about nursing.” “Write about welding.” Vagueness is the point of version 1; do not improve it yet.

Step 2 — write versions 2, 3 and 4

Each one adds at least one of the five anatomy elements to the version before it, and keeps everything the earlier version had. Use the volunteering ladder above as your model for the moves, not for the wording — your topic is different, so your context and constraints will be too.

Step 3 — run each version in a new chat

Open Gemini (or another tool if you prefer). Run version 1, then start a fresh conversation and run version 2, and again for 3 and 4. Four prompts, four separate chats.

Caution

Why this matters, and it genuinely does: if you run all four in one conversation, the tool answers version 4 having already seen versions 1 through 3. You would be measuring the conversation, not the prompt — and you would have no way to tell which one you were looking at. Next lesson does the one-conversation version on purpose. This one is the controlled comparison.

Step 4 — the verdict

Which version produced the most useful result, and why? Name the rung, and name the specific thing about that version’s request that made the difference. The most useful version is not always version 4 — if rung 3 got you what you needed and rung 4 added constraints that made it worse, say so. That is a real finding and it is the more interesting answer.
