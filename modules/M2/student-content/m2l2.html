The Same Trick, Four Times

Point last lesson’s idea at something other than words.

Read this section

Last lesson you learned what a language model does: it learned patterns from an enormous amount of text, and it produces new text that fits those patterns.

Now take that idea and point it at something other than words.

Learn patterns from an enormous number of pictures and you can produce new pictures. Do it with recorded speech and you can produce new speech. Do it with video and you can produce new clips. The material changes; the shape of the idea doesn’t. That’s what people mean by multimodal AI: AI systems that can process and generate multiple types of data (text, images, audio, video) within a single model or workflow.

The four areas are at genuinely different stages of maturity, though, and knowing which is which will save you a lot of wasted time.

Text

What it makes: writing, summaries, translation, code.

How mature it is: the most developed.

The classic tell: well-shaped but unchecked.

Images

What it makes: pictures from a written description.

How mature it is: widely usable, with known weak spots.

The classic tell: lettering and counts.

Audio

What it makes: speech, music, sound effects.

How mature it is: convincing in short passages.

The classic tell: emotional delivery.

Video

What it makes: short clips from a description.

How mature it is: newest, visibly unfinished.

The classic tell: things that change between frames.

Text

The most developed of the four.

Read this section

The most developed of the four, and the one you’ll use most in this course. Text generation covers writing and rewriting, summarizing long material down, translating between languages, and drafting code.

What text generation is unusually good at is shape: producing something in the right form. Ask for a cover letter and you get a thing correctly shaped like a cover letter. That’s a real convenience and also a real trap, because a well-shaped answer feels finished, and shape is not the same as correctness.

Caution

A well-shaped answer feels finished. Getting the form right — a cover letter that looks like a cover letter, a summary that reads like a summary — is the thing text generation is best at, and it is not the same as getting the content right. Module 3 is about the gap between those two.

Images

Subtraction, not construction.

Read this section

Image generation works differently from text generation, and the difference is worth understanding.

A text model builds its output forward, one piece at a time. An image model — usually a diffusion model — works by subtraction. It starts with a field of pure visual noise, like television static, and then removes a little of the noise, over and over, at each step nudging what’s left toward something that matches your description. After enough passes, an image has emerged from the static.

It’s a strange process to describe and a stranger one to watch, and the useful consequence is this: the model isn’t drawing, and it isn’t assembling your picture out of pieces of other pictures. It’s resolving noise toward a description. That’s why you can’t ask it to move one object slightly and get the same image back — there is no image to edit, only another run.

(You don’t need any mathematical detail here, and this course won’t give you any.)

Audio

Two rough families.

Read this section

Speech synthesis produces spoken audio from written text, and now does it with intonation and pacing convincing enough that the older robotic tell is gone. Music and sound generation produces instrumental passages, sung vocals, or sound effects from a description.

Synthesized speech that convincingly imitates a specific real person raises obvious questions about consent and impersonation. Module 4 takes that up properly — it’s a bigger topic than a bullet.

Video

It has to be right, and stay right.

Read this section

The newest of the four and the most visibly unfinished. Current tools produce short clips from a written description, and the results range from genuinely impressive to obviously wrong, often within the same clip.

The reason video is hard is that it has to be right and stay right. A generated image only has to hold together once. A generated video has to hold together across every frame — an object has to remain the same object, a hand has to keep the same number of fingers, and things have to fall the way things fall.

Where It Falls Over accurate as of September 2026

Failure patterns common enough to watch for.

Read this section
Verify

Don’t take this section’s word for it. Everything below describes September 2026, and these are the exact weaknesses that get worked on. If a claim here matters to a decision you’re making, spend two minutes testing it in the tool you actually use.

  • Images: written text inside a picture — signs, labels, book covers — is a standing weak spot, because letterforms have to be exactly right rather than approximately right. Hands, and anything else where a specific count matters, are the other classic tell.
  • Audio: synthesized speech tends to be strongest in short, neutral passages and weakest where emotional delivery or unusual names carry the meaning.
  • Video: watch for things that change identity mid-clip, physics that looks approximately right rather than right, and lettering, which fails for the same reason it fails in images.
  • All four: output tends to be most convincing where the request is generic and least convincing where it’s specific. The more particular your ask, the more you’ll notice the seams.

A caution about this list: it describes where things stood when this lesson was written, and these are precisely the failures that get worked on. A weak spot you read about is not a weak spot you’ve verified. Check the tool in front of you.

What It Costs You

Practical consequences you’ll run into.

Read this section

Generating an image, a piece of audio, or a video takes substantially more computing work than generating a paragraph of text. That has practical consequences:

  • Free tiers meter the four differently. Text is usually the most generous; images, audio and video are usually the most limited. Where exactly the limits sit changes often enough that you should read your own tool’s limits rather than trust anything written here.
  • Waiting is normal. Text arrives as it’s produced. Images take longer, and video longer still.
  • Not every tool does every one. Which of the four a given tool offers is a decision its makers made, and it changes. Checking is the skill. Assuming is the mistake — including assuming based on what a tool could do last term.

M2L2-A1 — What survived the trip

10 minutes Optional export

Write a sentence, turn it into a picture, and see what survived the trip.

Step 1 — everyone

Open Gemini (or another tool if you prefer) and generate one short paragraph on a topic of your choice. Use the prompt below or your own.

Write one short paragraph describing a farmers market on an early autumn morning.

Step 2

Pick the single most vivid sentence in that paragraph. That sentence is now your image description.

Step 3 — choose the path that fits what you have

Path A — your college account

Look at the tool you’re already signed into and find its image generation. It should be there. Tools change what they offer, though, so looking is the only way to actually know — and that check is part of this exercise, not a preliminary to it. Describe your sentence to it and let it generate. Then go to Step 4.

Path B — a free image-generation tool of your choice

If you already use one, or want to try one, use it. This is optional and always has been — nothing in this course requires an account with a company outside the college, and you lose no credit by not opening one.

Path C — no image tool, no account, full credit

Don’t generate anything. Instead, write down what you predict an image generator would do with your sentence: which details it would keep, which it would quietly drop, and which it would change or invent. Be specific and commit to your predictions before you read anyone else’s.

Step 4 — the comparison note

This is the actual assignment. In two or three sentences: what did the image preserve, what did it lose, and what did it change or add relative to your sentence? (Path C: what do you predict it would preserve, lose, and change — and which of your predictions are you least sure about?)
