One Conversation Instead of Four

The opposite of what M2L3 asked you to do, and on purpose.

Read this section

Last lesson you wrote four separate prompts and ran each in its own chat, deliberately keeping them from contaminating one another. That was a controlled comparison, and it answered a specific question: which construction was worth the effort.

This lesson does the opposite on purpose.

Here you send one prompt, read what comes back, and then keep going in the same conversation — because the answer you get is information you didn’t have when you wrote the prompt. You now know what it assumed, what it over-explained, what it skipped, and where it went generic. Every one of those is something to say next.

The reframe worth taking from this lesson is small and it changes how the whole thing feels: the first answer is not the deliverable. It is the first draft, and you are the editor. Most people treat a disappointing first answer as a verdict on the tool. It is closer to a rough draft handed to you by someone who has never met you and did not know what you were working on.

Four Moves

More options than “try again.”

Read this section

When an answer isn’t right, you have more options than “try again.”

Revise

What it does: names what to change, specifically, and leaves the rest alone.

What it sounds like: “Keep the structure but cut it by half.” “Same content, but write it for someone who has never taken a statistics course.”

When to reach for it: the answer is close and you can name the gap.

Narrow

What it does: takes one part of the answer and goes deeper on it.

What it sounds like: “Expand the third paragraph into its own section and drop the rest.”

When to reach for it: long answers are often one good paragraph surrounded by filler; narrowing is how you find out which paragraph it was.

Branch

What it does: asks for alternatives rather than an improvement.

What it sounds like: “Give me three different openings for this, in three different tones.”

When to reach for it: you don’t yet know what you want and would recognize it if you saw it — or the first answer was fine but felt inevitable.

Critique

What it does: asks it to turn on its own output.

What it sounds like: “What’s the weakest part of this?” “What would an unconvinced reader push back on first?” “What did I ask for that you didn’t quite deliver?”

When to reach for it: structural gaps, missing coverage and tonal problems — the things that are hard to see in your own draft.

Revise is the workhorse, and the specificity you practiced in M2L3 applies here too — “make it better” is as vague in round three as it was in round one. Branch is the one most people forget they have: three options tell you whether the first answer was actually the obvious choice or just the first one. And critique works better than it has any right to for finding the shape problems in a draft.

Caution

A critique is another answer, not a check. When you ask a tool what’s wrong with its own output, what comes back is generated the same way the output was. That makes it genuinely useful for structure, coverage and tone — and it is not evidence that anything in the draft is true. Module 3 is about that second question, and it deserves the whole module it gets.

Knowing When to Stop

Iteration is not free and it is not monotonic.

Read this section

More rounds do not reliably mean a better result, and there are two failure shapes worth recognizing while you are in one.

The conversation drifts. Ten turns in, the answer has accumulated every adjustment you asked for and no longer resembles anything you would have wanted from the start. Each individual change was an improvement; the sum is a camel. When this happens, the move is not another round — it is to write one fresh prompt containing everything you have learned you wanted, and start clean.

The returns flatten. Rounds four through eight produce differently-worded versions of the same answer. That usually means the request has been fully specified and you have what this approach is going to give you. Recognizing that moment is a skill, and it is the one that saves the most time.

Both of these are worth writing down when they happen to you, because “round three made it worse” is a genuine finding about your own process, not a failure to report.

Adapting to the Work

The five parts don’t change between fields. What you put in them does.

Read this section

The differences are more specific than “be professional.”

Academic writing

The heavy lifting is in context and constraints: the audience’s level, the argument you are actually making, the evidence you are supplying yourself, and what you do not want assumed.

What you disclose about AI assistance, and how you cite it, is Module 3’s subject — not a prompting question, and it is treated properly there.

Technical documentation

Precision about the reader is everything: what they already know, what they must not be assumed to know, and what happens if they follow a step wrong.

Format carries unusual weight here — numbered steps, prerequisites first, expected result stated after each action.

Creative work

Constraints do more than they do anywhere else, and the useful ones are usually negative: what to avoid, what has been done to death, which register to stay out of.

Branching is the natural move in creative work; revising a single line toward “better” rarely is.

Data analysis

Describe the shape of what you have in words — how many rows, what each column means, where the gaps are — before asking anything.

Ask for the approach before the answer: “What would you look at first, and why?” is a better opening than “what does this data show?”, which invites a confident answer built on a structure you never saw.

What you paste into a tool is a decision, and you already have the framework for it from M1L5.

Prompts Worth Keeping

The practical payoff of everything in this module.

Read this section

When a conversation finally lands somewhere good, you are holding two things: a result, and a recipe. The result you use once. The recipe you keep.

Take everything the conversation accumulated and collapse it into one standalone prompt — the one you would have written at the start if you had known. That collapsed prompt is a template, and it is reusable in a way the conversation is not.

Role: You are [who this should be written as].
Task: [the verb, and what it acts on].
Context: [what you would have to tell a new colleague before they could start].
Constraints: [length, reading level, what to include, what to avoid].
Format: [the shape of the answer].

Filled in, from a conversation that took six rounds to get right:

Role: You are an academic advisor who has worked with first-generation college
students for years.
Task: Write a reply to a student email asking whether to drop a class they are
failing at midterm.
Context: The student is in their first semester, taking twelve units, and works
about twenty-five hours a week. They have not said which class or why. Our
withdrawal deadline is in nine days.
Constraints: Under 200 words. Warm but not soft - do not reassure them that it
will be fine. Ask the two questions I would need answered before advising either
way. Do not recommend dropping or staying.
Format: An email, no subject line, no salutation placeholder.

Notice that almost nothing in the filled-in version could have been guessed. The nine-day deadline, the twenty-five hours, the refusal to recommend — those came out of the conversation, one at a time, and the template is where they end up. This is the shape of the thing you should be saving. Not the answers. The requests that produced them.

Note

Save the request, not the reply. A good answer solves today. A good prompt solves the next twenty times you need this. If you keep one thing from Module 2, keep a file of prompts that worked.

When to Switch Tools

Three situations, none of which is about one product being better than another.

Read this section

Sometimes the right move isn’t another round in this conversation.

The thread has gone stale. You have drifted or flattened out, as above. The cheapest fix is a new conversation in the same tool, using your collapsed prompt — and it is the one to try first, because most of the value of “switching” is really the value of starting clean.

You want an attempt that hasn’t seen any of this. Everything in your thread is now context, including your false starts. Running your collapsed prompt somewhere with no history of the conversation tells you what the prompt does on its own.

You want to know whether a result is an artifact of one tool. If two tools given the identical prompt produce recognizably similar answers, that is worth something. If they produce very different ones, that is worth more — and it is a question about your prompt at least as often as it is about them.

What is not on this list is “because tool X is better at Y.” Which of these tools does what, and how well, changes on a timescale of months, and anything you read about it — here included — is out of date faster than you would think. What doesn’t go stale is the habit: run the same prompt in two places and read the difference yourself.

M2L4-A1 — One task, one conversation, one keeper

35 minutes Portfolio 04

Take one real task through a conversation, collapse what you learn into a single prompt, and see what that prompt does somewhere else.

Step 1 — pick a real task

Choose something from your “10 Things AI can help me do in my daily life” list — the one you drafted in our first session — or a task from your own field or coursework. It has to be something you actually want done; a made-up task produces a made-up conversation and there is nothing to learn from it.

Your 10-Things list is yours and stays yours — you’re not turning the list in or revising it here, and we come back to it at the end of the semester. Only the one task you pick appears in the PDF you file.

Step 2 — write your opening prompt

Use M2L3’s five parts. You do not need all five. You do need to have decided about each of them.

Step 3 — three rounds, one conversation

Run your prompt in Gemini (or another tool if you prefer), then keep going in that same conversation for at least three more turns, using the four moves. Mix them — three revisions in a row is one move used three times, not three moves.

For each round, record what you asked for and what actually changed. “Nothing changed” and “it got worse” are real answers and are worth more than a polite “it improved.” If a round made it worse, say which move it was and what happened.

Step 4 — collapse it

Write the single standalone prompt you would use if you were starting this task over, knowing everything the conversation taught you. This is your consolidated prompt. It should be able to stand alone with no conversation behind it.

Step 5 — run it somewhere else

Open the other AI tool your college account gives you — you have access to it through your @student.mendocino.edu email — and run your consolidated prompt there, in a fresh conversation.

Or any free tool you already use, if you prefer. Nothing in this course requires you to open an account with a company outside the college.

Step 6 — compare the two outputs

In two or three sentences: how did the second result differ from where your conversation ended up, and what does that difference tell you about your prompt?

Note

Compare the two outputs, not the two products. You ran one prompt twice. What that tells you is how much of your result came from the prompt and how much came from the six turns of conversation behind it — which is a fact about your work, and portable. Whether one tool is “better” is a much bigger claim than one prompt can support, and it would be out of date by the time you finished writing it down.

Check Yourself

Not graded, not recorded. Three questions.

Open the three questions
1. You’ve been refining in one conversation for ten turns. The answer now contains every adjustment you asked for and no longer resembles anything you’d have wanted at the start. What’s the move?
2. You ask a tool “what’s the weakest part of this draft?” and it gives you a thoughtful critique. What have you learned?
3. Your conversation finally produced a result you’re happy with. What’s worth saving?
