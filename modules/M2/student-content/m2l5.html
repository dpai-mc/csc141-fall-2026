Two Ways a Model Reaches You

The question is not how good a model is. It is where the computation happens.

Read this section

Everything you have used in this course so far works the same way from where you sit: you type into a box in your browser, and an answer comes back. That sameness hides the decision this lesson is about.

The question is not how good a model is. It is where the computation happens, and who hands you the model in the first place. There are two answers, and almost everything else in this lesson follows from which one you are dealing with.

A frontier model is a large, state-of-the-art AI model offered as a closed, cloud-hosted service — highest capability, data leaves the user’s device. You reach it through a service. The weights — the enormous set of numbers that are the trained model — stay with the company that made them. You never have a copy; you have permission to send it things. (Examples: Gemini, GPT, Claude.)

An open-weight model, also called a local model when you are the one running it, is a model whose weights are openly distributed and can run on hardware the user controls — trading capability for privacy, cost, and control. Someone published the file. You can download it. What you do with it after that is between you and your own computer. (Examples: Llama, Mistral.)

Model examples accurate as of 2026-09. Which category a particular model sits in is a decision its makers took and can take again — weights get published, and sometimes stop being published.

Those two definitions are the ones this course uses everywhere, including in Module 4. They are worth reading twice, because almost every disagreement you will hear about “AI” turns out to be a disagreement about which of these two someone is picturing.

The words point at two different things, which is why they get confused. “Frontier” and “open-weight” describe how you get the model. “Local” describes where it runs. An open-weight model sitting on a company’s server in another state is open-weight but not local to you. That is not a technicality — it is the whole of the privacy question later in this lesson.

Caution

“Open-weight” and “local” are not the same word. Open-weight describes how the model was published. Local describes where it is running. An openly-published model running on a company’s servers is one and not the other — and the privacy argument later in this lesson depends entirely on the second, not the first.

The Four Questions

Four separate questions that happen to have the same two candidate answers.

Read this section

When people argue about this, they are almost always arguing about one of four things without saying which. Naming them separately is most of the skill.

Capability — how much model there is. The largest models need more hardware to run than a person owns, so the organizations that own that hardware are the ones offering them as a service. That is an economic fact about scale, not a verdict about any particular model. The gap between the two categories on everyday tasks has been narrowing, and by how much is a moving number — which is why this lesson does not print one, and why you should distrust anyone who does without a date on it.

Privacy — what crosses the network. This is the axis that actually changes what you can do. When the computation happens on someone else’s computers, your prompt goes there. Everything in it goes there: the paragraph you pasted, the document you uploaded, the question you were slightly embarrassed to ask. What happens to it after that is governed by terms you accepted rather than negotiated. When the computation happens on hardware you control, it doesn’t leave. Module 4 comes back to this as something an organization can do on purpose.

Cost — who pays, and in what. A hosted service has no hardware for you to buy; you pay a subscription, a per-use price, an institutional account, or you accept the limits of a free tier. Running a model yourself inverts it: the hardware is the cost, up front and yours, and after that the marginal question is electricity. Neither is simply cheaper. Which one wins depends entirely on how much you use it, which is a question about you, not about the models.

Control — whether it can change under you. A hosted model is the version the provider is serving today. It can be updated, and it can be withdrawn, and you generally find out when your results do. A model file you have downloaded stays exactly the model you downloaded, for as long as you keep it, whether or not anyone is still supporting it. That cuts both ways: nothing improves either.

Capability

The question it asks: how much model is there?

Why it follows from where the computation happens: the biggest models need hardware a person doesn’t own, so they live where that hardware is.

Privacy

The question it asks: what leaves your device?

Why it follows from where the computation happens: if the work happens elsewhere, the material has to travel there first.

Cost

The question it asks: who pays, and in what currency?

Why it follows from where the computation happens: someone has to own the hardware — either you buy it once, or you rent it by the use.

Control

The question it asks: can it change under you?

Why it follows from where the computation happens: whoever runs it decides which version runs, and for how long.

The four are not a scorecard, and there is no total. They are four separate questions that happen to have the same two candidate answers. Which one matters is a property of your task — and that is the thing to actually take away from this lesson.

Verify

The capability gap is the part of this lesson most likely to be out of date. Every number anyone gives you for it — how close an openly-distributed model gets to the largest hosted ones — is a snapshot with a date on it, and the date is usually missing. When you meet that claim, look for when it was written before you decide what to do with it.

What “On Your Own Hardware” Actually Means

“You can just run it yourself” is said far more often than it is done.

Read this section

It is worth being concrete about this, because “you can just run it yourself” is said far more often than it is done.

What it takes. A model file is a download measured in gigabytes, sometimes tens of them. It wants a machine with a lot of memory, and it is much happier with the kind of graphics hardware sold for gaming and professional 3D work than with the processor in an ordinary laptop. Getting it running is usually a software-installation exercise, and often a command-line one. None of that is exotic — people do it in their spare rooms — but none of it is opening a browser tab either.

What you get

Something that answers when the internet is down. Something that is not metered, not logged anywhere but your own machine, and not going to change next Tuesday.

For a confidential document, a huge repetitive job, or a place with bad connectivity, those are not small advantages.

What you give up

The very largest models, mostly. Speed, generally, unless the hardware is serious.

And the quiet convenience of somebody else keeping the thing running, which is worth more than it sounds.

Note

This week’s activity is research, not installation. You will not download or run anything. You are finding out enough about one openly-distributed model to fill in four cells — which is the same thing you would do before recommending one, and it is the part of the job that transfers.

Nothing in this course asks you to install one. You are learning to read a trade-off that institutions, employers and software you use are making on your behalf all the time. You do not have to have run a model yourself to notice that a vendor chose one of these two — and to ask which, and why.

Reading the Trade-off

A small habit that dissolves a remarkable amount of disagreement.

Read this section

Here is the useful habit, and it is a small one.

When you meet a claim about “AI” — in a product announcement, a policy, a news story, a colleague’s confident opinion — ask which of the two it is talking about, and which of the four questions it is really answering. A remarkable amount of disagreement dissolves at that point, because the two people are answering different questions and neither said which.

Two examples of the move:

  • “AI is a privacy disaster.” Which category? The claim is largely about the first one, and it is largely about the privacy axis, and it is largely true there. It says very little about the second.
  • “You can run this stuff on a laptop now.” Which model? Doing what? This is a capability claim, it is the most time-sensitive kind of claim in the field, and the honest version of it always has a date attached.

You already own the answer to one of these. Every tool you have used in this course so far sits in the first category — which is a fact about the tools, not a judgement about them, and it is the reason the privacy questions in this course have the shape they do.

Step 1 — pick one openly-distributed model

Any one. Two families are named in this lesson’s definitions if you want a starting point, and there are many others. You are not installing it, and you do not need a computer that could run it.

Step 2 — find out enough to fill four cells

Open Gemini (or another tool if you prefer) and find out, for your model: roughly how big the download is and what hardware it wants; what happens to a prompt you give it when you run it yourself; what it costs to obtain and to run; and whether the version you download can change afterwards.

Step 3 — write the four cells

One or two sentences each, in the same terms as the pre-filled column. Where the honest answer is “it depends,” say what it depends on — that is a better cell than a confident one.

Step 4 — name your weakest cell

Which of your four are you least sure about, and what would you need to find out to be sure? One sentence. Being able to point at the soft spot in your own summary is worth more here than four tidy answers.
