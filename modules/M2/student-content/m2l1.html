From Sorting to Making

Why Module 2 is a different machine, not a bigger one.

Read this section

In Module 1 you met AI systems that sort things. A spam filter decides: spam or not spam. A recommendation engine decides: you’ll probably like this, you probably won’t. The dataset you analyzed in week 3 was training data for exactly that kind of system — one that takes something in and puts a label on it.

This module is about a different kind of system. Instead of putting a label on something that already exists, it makes something that didn’t exist before. You type a request and get back a paragraph, an image, a piece of audio — content that isn’t retrieved from anywhere, but generated.

That’s the whole distinction, and it’s worth holding onto, because almost every AI tool you’ve heard of in the last few years is on the “making” side.

What “Generative AI” Means

The definition, and the shape of the question each kind of system answers.

Read this section

Generative AI is: AI systems that create new content (text, images, audio, video) rather than classifying or analyzing existing data. Includes large language models (LLMs) and diffusion models.

Compare that to the systems from Module 1, which are sometimes called discriminative — they discriminate between categories. Here’s the difference in the shape of the question each one answers:

  • A sorting system answers: “Which of these fixed options does this belong to?” Spam or not spam. Five stars or one star. Cat or dog. The set of possible answers exists before the question is asked.
  • A generating system answers: “What would plausibly come next?” And the set of possible answers is effectively unlimited — which is why two people asking the same question get two different paragraphs, and why you asking the same question twice gets two different paragraphs. You saw that happen in week 1, in M1L1-A1, before you had a name for why.

Sorting (discriminative)

The question it answers: “Which of these fixed options does this belong to?”

Possible answers: a fixed list, decided in advance.

Examples: spam filter, recommendation engine, the news-topic tagging in your week-3 dataset.

Making (generative)

The question it answers: “What would plausibly come next?”

Possible answers: effectively unlimited.

Examples: writing a paragraph, generating an image, drafting an email.

Neither kind is more advanced than the other; they’re built for different jobs. A bank would much rather have a system that reliably sorts transactions into “fraud” and “not fraud” than one that writes a lovely paragraph about your transaction.

Large Language Models

Both halves of the name are doing work.

Read this section

Most of the generative tools you’ll use in this course are built on a Large Language Model (LLM): a type of generative AI trained on massive text corpora to predict and generate human-like text.

“Language model” is the older and more important half. A language model is a system that has learned, from enormous amounts of text, which pieces of text tend to follow which other pieces. That’s it. That’s the whole job description. Give it some text, and it produces what would plausibly come next.

“Large” refers to scale in two directions: the amount of text it learned from, and the number of internal values it adjusted while learning. Both are big enough that the numbers stop being intuitive, and both keep changing — which is why this lesson doesn’t give you figures. What matters isn’t the size, it’s that the size is what made the difference. Language models have existed for decades; the recent ones are not a new idea so much as the same idea at a scale that started working.

How It Actually Produces Text

The part that surprises people.

Read this section

When you ask an LLM a question, it does not look up an answer. It doesn’t have a database of facts to consult. What it does is much stranger: it takes everything you wrote, and produces the piece of text most likely to come next. Then it takes what you wrote plus that piece, and produces the next piece. Then again. And again — until it produces a piece that means “I’m done.”

The pieces are called tokens. Tokenization is: how models break text into small units for processing. A token is often a word, but it can be part of a word, a space, or a punctuation mark. You don’t need to know how the splitting works — only that the model is working in these small chunks rather than in whole sentences or whole ideas.

At each step, the model isn’t picking one obvious continuation. It’s scoring every token it knows as a possible next piece, and choosing from among the likely ones. That’s why the output varies between runs: there’s genuine selection happening at every step, not a lookup returning a stored result.

Sit with the consequence of that for a second. A model produces text that is likely, not text that has been checked. Those two things overlap a lot — likely text is usually true text, which is why these tools are useful at all — but they are not the same property, and they come apart in ways you’ll spend all of Module 3 learning to catch.

Caution

“Likely” and “true” are different properties. They agree most of the time, which is what makes these tools worth using — and when they disagree, the text looks exactly the same. Module 3 is where you learn to tell.

Where the Model’s “Knowledge” Comes From: Two Stages

Patterns, then manners.

Read this section

Models are built in two distinct stages, and knowing the difference explains a lot of tool behavior.

Stage 1 — Pre-training. The model is exposed to an enormous, broad body of text and learns to predict what comes next in it. Nobody is telling it what’s correct or teaching it tasks. It is absorbing patterns of language — grammar, style, how arguments are structured, and, unavoidably, the patterns in whatever it was shown. This is where the bulk of the effort goes, and it’s the stage that makes everything from Module 1 relevant: the training data’s gaps become the model’s gaps, exactly as you found in your dataset analysis.

Stage 2 — Fine-tuning. The broad model is then trained further on a much smaller, deliberately chosen set of examples, to shape how it behaves: to answer questions rather than continue them, to follow instructions, to adopt a tone, to decline certain requests. Fine-tuning doesn’t teach the model the world. It teaches it the job.

A useful way to hold the two apart: pre-training is where a model gets its patterns; fine-tuning is where it gets its manners.

Not Just Text

Four areas, one family resemblance.

Read this section

Generative AI isn’t only language. The same basic idea — learn patterns from a very large amount of examples, then produce new things that fit those patterns — has been applied across four broad areas:

  • Text — writing, summarizing, translating, drafting code.
  • Images — producing pictures from a written description.
  • Audio — synthesized speech, music, sound effects.
  • Video — short generated clips.

Next lesson takes each of these apart: how image generation actually works, what each area currently does well, and where the output visibly falls over. For now, all you need is that the four exist and that they share a family resemblance.
