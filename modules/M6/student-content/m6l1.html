What “AI Governance” Means

Who sets the rules for AI, and how.

Read this section

For five modules you’ve looked at AI one use at a time: how it works, how to prompt it, how to check it, where it’s biased, what it does with your data, what it means for your job. Module 6 steps back to ask who decides what AI is allowed to do — and how you can have a say.

AI governance. The frameworks, policies, regulations, and practices that guide the development, deployment, and use of AI systems. Includes government regulation, industry standards, and ethical frameworks.

Governance happens at several levels at once:

  • Laws and regulations — rules a government can enforce, with penalties.
  • Standards and frameworks — guidance that organizations follow voluntarily, often because customers, insurers or courts expect it. The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology’s AI Risk Management Framework (2023) is a widely used example.
  • Organizational policies — the rules an employer, a hospital or a college writes for its own people. A course’s rules on AI use for assignments are one.
  • Personal codes — the rules you write for yourself. You wrote one in Module 3, and you’ll revise it this module.

Why now? Because AI is already making or shaping decisions about people — who gets screened in for a job (Module 4), what a doctor’s note says (Module 5) — and the rules for those decisions are being written right now, in different places, in different ways.

Three Approaches to Writing the Rules

Most AI rules lean on one of three ideas.

Read this section

In M4L5 you used three lenses to judge one use of AI. Governments and organizations face a bigger version of the same problem: writing rules that will apply to thousands of uses they can’t see in advance. That’s the job of an ethical framework at scale.

Ethical framework (for AI). A structured set of principles used to evaluate the moral implications of AI development and use. Examples: risk-based frameworks, human-centered design principles, transparency requirements.

Those three examples are the three approaches you’ll see most often:

Risk-based

Stricter rules for riskier uses

The idea
Sort AI uses by how much harm they could do. Ban the worst, regulate the risky heavily, and leave low-risk uses mostly alone.
Example
The European Union’s AI Act: a short list of banned practices, strict requirements for “high-risk” uses like hiring and credit, and lighter duties for everything else.
The hard part
Someone has to decide what counts as high-risk — and uses change faster than the list.

Principle-based

Shared values, applied case by case

The idea
Agree on broad principles — human rights, fairness, transparency, safety, accountability — and expect everyone building or using AI to apply them.
Example
The OECD AI Principles (2019, updated 2024), backed by 47 governments, and human-centered design principles many companies publish.
The hard part
Principles are easy to agree on and hard to enforce. They say what to aim for, not what happens if you miss.

Transparency-based

Make it visible, then let people respond

The idea
Require developers and users of AI to disclose what they’re doing — publish safety plans, report incidents, tell people when AI is involved — and let the public, courts and markets respond.
Example
California’s SB 53 (2025) requires the largest AI developers to publish safety frameworks and report serious incidents. Colorado’s 2026 law requires telling people when AI helps make a major decision about them.
The hard part
Disclosure only helps if someone reads it and can act on it.

Real rules mix all three. And the two scales connect: a rule written at scale still ends in individual cases, where M4L5’s questions — harms and benefits, rights, fairness — are the ones that matter.

Where Things Stand

The main rules affecting AI in the U.S. and Europe, as of October 2026.

Read this section

European Union

The EU AI Act is the most comprehensive AI law so far. Its bans on certain practices have applied since February 2025, and its rules for general-purpose AI models since August 2025. In 2026 the EU delayed its rules for high-risk uses — to December 2027 for uses like hiring, and August 2028 for AI built into regulated products.

Gibson Dunn, 2026-05-27; Digital Omnibus in force 2026-07-27.

U.S. federal government

There is no comprehensive federal AI law. A December 2025 executive order directed federal agencies to challenge state AI laws seen as burdensome, and in March 2026 the White House recommended that Congress pass one national framework that would override state laws. As of October 2026, Congress has not passed it. A September 2026 order changed the term federal agencies use, from “Artificial Intelligence” to “Super Intelligence,” without changing what’s covered.

Executive Order 14365 (2025-12-11); Ropes & Gray, 2026-03-30; Executive Order 14434 (2026-09-29), Wiley, 2026-10-02.

U.S. states

States have passed their own laws. California’s SB 53 (signed September 2025) requires the largest AI developers to publish safety frameworks, report critical safety incidents to the state, and protect employees who report risks. Colorado paused enforcement of its 2024 AI law during a federal lawsuit and replaced it with a narrower law, effective January 2027, centered on telling people when AI helped make a major decision about them.

National Law Review, 2026-07-09; JD Supra, 2026-05-28.

Standards

Voluntary frameworks fill much of the gap. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework asks organizations to govern, map, measure and manage AI risks, and many companies and agencies use it as their reference point — though nothing requires it.

NIST, AI RMF 1.0, January 2023.

One national rulebook, or room for states?

The biggest open question in U.S. AI policy is whether federal law should override state AI laws. Both sides make serious arguments.

The case for one national framework

  • AI products cross state lines instantly; fifty different rulebooks are expensive to follow and may slow development.
  • Smaller companies can’t afford a separate compliance team for every state, which favors the largest firms.
  • National security and international competition, its supporters argue, call for one consistent national approach.

The case for letting states act

  • Congress hasn’t passed a comprehensive law, and states are protecting residents in the meantime.
  • States have long served as testing grounds for policy that later goes national.
  • Overriding state laws without a federal replacement, critics argue, would leave some harms with no rule at all.

Which approach is better is exactly the kind of question your policy brief can take a position on — with evidence, and with the other side’s best argument answered.

AI law is changing month to month. Everything in this section is accurate as of October 2026. Before you rely on any of it — in your policy brief or anywhere else — check the current status in a dated source.

Different Fields, Different Rules

The same technology faces very different rules depending on where it’s used.

Read this section
  • Healthcare AI is regulated much like other medical devices. In M5L4 you saw that the U.S. Food and Drug Administration has authorized more than 1,400 AI-enabled devices — each reviewed before it could be sold. Patient-privacy law applies to the data.
  • Consumer chatbots face few AI-specific rules in the U.S.; they’re governed mostly by general consumer-protection and privacy law, and by each company’s own terms — the terms you read in your Module 4 security assessment.
  • Hiring, lending and housing fall under existing anti-discrimination law whether or not AI is involved — the Module 4 screening case is a legal question as well as an ethical one.
  • Autonomous systems like self-driving vehicles and drones are governed by transportation and aviation regulators, often through permits and testing rules that vary by state or country.

So “is AI regulated?” has no single answer. The useful question is the one you practiced in M5L4: for this use, in this field, what rules already apply — and what’s missing?

The Gap

The technology moves faster than the rules.

Read this section

A new AI capability can reach millions of people in a few months. A law typically takes years to draft, pass and put into effect — and then it can be delayed, challenged in court or replaced. You’ve seen all three in this lesson: the EU delayed its high-risk rules, Colorado’s first law was paused by a lawsuit and rewritten, and the U.S. still has no comprehensive federal law.

That gap is sometimes called the pacing problem. It means that, for now, much of the responsibility for using AI well falls on organizations and individuals — on the workflows you designed in Module 5 and the Code you’re about to revise. It also means public pressure matters: in many places, the rules that exist came from people asking for them, which is where M6L2 picks up.

M6L1-A1 — One rule, checked

30 minutes Optional export

Research one current AI law, regulation or framework, check what the AI tool told you against an official source, and compare its approach with the three in this lesson.

Step 1 — choose one

Choose one of these, or another AI law or policy you’re curious about — a state law, another country’s, or a policy at a company or college:

  • The EU AI Act
  • California’s SB 53 (Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act)
  • Colorado’s SB 26-189 (its 2026 AI law)
  • The NIST AI Risk Management Framework
  • The OECD AI Principles

Step 2 — ask the tool

Open Gemini (or another tool if you prefer) in a new chat. Copy the prompt, fill in your choice, and send it.

Explain [the law or policy] in plain language for a college student. Tell me: what it covers, who it applies to, one specific requirement it creates, whether it is in effect today and since when, and a link to the official text or an official summary.

Step 3 — check two facts

Pick two specific facts from the answer — a date, a requirement, who it applies to. Find each in the official source or a dated news report. AI law changes fast, and tools are often out of date about it; an answer that was true last year is one of M3L1’s kinds of wrong.

Step 4 — compare

Which of the three approaches does it lean on most — risk-based, principle-based or transparency-based? And which M4L5 lens does it seem most concerned with?
