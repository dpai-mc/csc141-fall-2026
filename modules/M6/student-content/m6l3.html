What’s Coming

Three directions the technology is moving — described as ideas, not products.

Read this section

Predictions about AI date quickly, so this section describes directions, not products. Any of them could look different by the time you read this.

Many kinds of input at once

Multimodal AI. AI systems that can process and generate multiple types of data (text, images, audio, video) within a single model or workflow.

You’ve already used some of this. The direction is toward tools that see, hear and speak as fluently as they write — which makes them more useful, and makes deepfakes (M4L4) easier to produce.

AI that acts on its own

Agentic AI. AI systems designed to autonomously pursue goals, make decisions, and take actions with minimal human direction.

In M3L1 you met agentic browsing: a tool that clicks and fills things in on a web page for you instead of answering questions about it. In M5L2 you met AI as an agent with human oversight. The direction is toward more steps between human checks — booking, buying, sending, filing. Every question from M5L2 gets harder: who checks, when, and who answers for a mistake made three steps ago?

Learning plus rules

Some researchers are combining the pattern-learning of today’s models with explicit rules and logic — sometimes called neuro-symbolic AI. The hope is systems that can explain their reasoning and follow hard constraints. It’s mostly a research direction for now.

Caution

An agent acting for you is acting with your accounts. When a tool can send email, make purchases or submit forms in your name, a mistake — or a malicious web page that tricks it — happens with your identity attached. The more a tool can do on its own, the more the M5L2 questions matter.

Augment or Replace?

The question from Module 5, at the scale of society — and of your own mind.

Read this section

M5L1 looked at what has happened to jobs so far. Looking forward, forecasts disagree widely, and every one of them is a guess about choices people haven’t made yet. Whether AI mostly augments people or mostly replaces them isn’t only a fact about the technology. It depends on how employers design jobs, what schools teach, and what rules governments write — the subject of this module.

The same question applies to your own thinking. A cognitive prosthetic is a tool that takes over part of a mental task — the way a calculator handles arithmetic or a map app handles directions. Used well, it frees your attention for harder work. You’ve also seen the cost: M3L2’s cognitive offloading, and M5L2’s doctors whose skill faded when the tool wasn’t there.

Further out are technologies that connect to the body directly. Brain–computer interfaces — devices that read signals from the brain — are being tested to help people with paralysis control a computer. They raise new questions that the lenses from M4L5 can start on: who owns the data from your brain, and what consent could mean for it?

Try this

Name one thing you’ve stopped doing yourself because a tool does it — directions, spelling, arithmetic, remembering phone numbers. Was that a good trade? Would you make the same trade for writing, or for judgment at work?

The Costs Nobody Puts on the Price Tag

Electricity, water and the places data centers are built.

Read this section

Every AI answer runs on computers in a data center somewhere, and those computers use electricity and, often, water for cooling. According to the International Energy Agency, data centers used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024 — about 1.5% of all the electricity used worldwide, with the U.S. using the largest share. The IEA projects that will more than double, to around 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, with AI as the main driver.

International Energy Agency, Energy and AI, 2025. The 2030 figure is a projection.

Those costs land somewhere specific. Communities near new data centers see effects on power prices, water supply and land use — and decide, through their local governments, whether to approve them. That makes energy a governance question too: who bears the cost of an AI answer, and who gets to decide? Fairness, from M4L5, asks whether the costs fall on the same group every time.

What Are You Actually Paying For?

A way to decide — not a recommendation.

Read this section

When this course ends, you’ll decide for yourself which AI tools to keep using and whether to pay for any. People currently pay for AI in three main ways:

A subscription to one company’s tool

You get that company’s best model and the features around it. You’re betting that one company stays good at what you need.

A tool that brings several together

One subscription that sends your questions to several underlying models, or pairs search with generation — like the answer engines in M3L1. You trade “the best of one” for access to several, and you may not control which model answers you.

Stacking free tiers

Using several tools’ free versions and switching when you hit a limit. It costs nothing but attention — and it’s what most people in this course did all semester.

None of these is the right answer. Four questions decide which fits you:

  1. How much do you actually use it? A few times a week and a few hours a day are different problems.
  2. Does it matter to you which model answers? For some tasks it does; for many it doesn’t.
  3. How much does checking sources matter in your work? If a lot, a tool that searches and shows sources by design is worth more to you.
  4. What would you lose if a tool you rely on changed its free tier tomorrow? That’s your real dependency.

Your answers to these belong in your revised AI Code. For current limits and terms, see the free-tier section of each tool guide — each has its own “accurate as of” date. Prices aren’t listed here because they change faster than this page can.

One more thing you now get to decide for yourself: your college account. Your access to Gemini and Copilot through your student account lasts only as long as the college keeps that account open — check the college’s policy for when accounts close after you leave. The protections that come with it (M1L5) end with the account.

Thinking in Scenarios

Not predicting the future — preparing for more than one.

Read this section

Nobody knows how AI will develop, and anyone who sounds certain is guessing. Organizations deal with that uncertainty with scenario planning: instead of one prediction, they describe several possible futures and ask what they’d do in each.

Best case

What would have to go right? Who benefits, and how widely?

Worst case

What would have to go wrong? Who is harmed first?

Most likely

Given what you’ve seen so far, what’s the realistic middle? What evidence is it based on?

Then the useful question: what guardrails would make the best case more likely and the worst case less? That’s where governance and your own choices meet.

M6L3-A1 — One future, half a page

20 minutes Optional export

Explore one emerging AI application or change, then write half a page: what changes, who benefits, who’s at risk, and what guardrails it needs.

Step 1 — pick a change

An agent that handles some part of your life or work for you; AI in your Module 5 field ten years from now; AI tutors in every classroom; a brain–computer interface; a new data center in your county — or your own idea.

Step 2 — explore it

Open Gemini (or another tool if you prefer) in a new chat. Copy the prompt, fill it in, and send it. Treat the answer as a starting point, not an authority — it’s describing a future nobody knows.

Describe three possible futures for [the change] over the next ten years: a best case, a worst case and a most likely case. For each, say who benefits and who is at risk. Keep each to three sentences, and say which parts are based on evidence and which are speculation.

Step 3 — write your own half page

In your own words, not the tool’s. Disagree with it where you think it’s wrong.
