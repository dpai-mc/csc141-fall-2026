Governing a Technology Without Borders

Why no single government can write the rules for AI on its own.

Read this section

In M6L1 you saw that the EU, the U.S. federal government and individual states are writing different rules. That raises a harder question: what happens when an AI model is trained in one country, run on servers in a second, and used by people in a third?

  • The product crosses borders; the law doesn’t. A rule in one country binds companies selling there, not the ones selling elsewhere — though large companies often adopt the strictest market’s rules everywhere rather than build different versions.
  • Countries want different things. Some put individual rights first, some economic growth, some national security or social stability. Those priorities lead to genuinely different rules, not just slower or faster ones.
  • Nobody wants to fall behind. Each government worries that strict rules will push AI development to countries with looser ones. That pressure pulls toward fewer rules everywhere, and it’s why international agreements are hard to reach.

The International Layer

Shared principles, one treaty, and a new UN forum.

Read this section

OECD AI Principles

Five shared principles — inclusive growth and well-being; human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy; transparency and explainability; robustness, security and safety; and accountability. Adopted in 2019 and updated in 2024, with 47 adherent governments. Voluntary: they guide, they don’t bind.

OECD.AI, accessed 2026-10.

Council of Europe AI treaty

The Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence, signed in September 2024 by countries including the U.S., the U.K. and the EU, is the first international treaty on AI. It commits signers to keeping AI consistent with human rights, democracy and the rule of law. A treaty binds a country only once that country ratifies it.

Council of Europe, signed 2024-09-05.

United Nations

In August 2025 the UN General Assembly created two bodies: an Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, which gathers evidence on how AI is changing society, and a Global Dialogue on AI Governance, where governments discuss what to do about it. The Dialogue first met in Geneva in July 2026. Neither can make binding rules.

UN General Assembly resolution A/RES/79/325, 2025-08-26.

Notice the pattern: the further up you go, the more agreement there is on principles — and the less power anyone has to enforce them. Most enforceable AI rules are still national, state or local.

Accurate as of 2026-10.

Where You Come In

The closer the decision, the more one person can affect it.

Read this section

You can’t vote at the UN. But many AI decisions that affect you directly are made much closer to home — by a city council, a school board, a college, an employer — and those are decisions ordinary people do change.

  • Public comment. City councils, county boards and state agencies must give the public a chance to speak or write before many decisions. Comments become part of the public record, and officials read them.
  • Showing up early. Agendas are posted before meetings. An item is easiest to change before the vote, not after.
  • Writing to the person who decides. A short, specific message to the right official — or to a manager, a dean or a board — with one clear request. That’s what a policy brief is.
  • Organizing. Professional associations, student government, unions and community groups carry more weight than one voice alone.
  • Voting for the people who make these decisions, at every level.
Note

A “consent calendar” is a list of routine items a council approves in one vote, without discussion. Many purchases appear there. Any council member can usually ask to pull an item off it for separate discussion, and residents can ask a council member to do that — or speak during public comment.

A Case Close to Home: Cameras That Read License Plates

The privacy question from our in-class discussion, decided in council chambers.

Read this section

In our in-class discussion of license-plate-reading cameras, you looked at maps showing where they are around Ukiah. Decisions about those cameras are made by city councils — and in the last year, some of those decisions changed because residents spoke up.

Flagstaff, Arizona (December 2025). After residents raised concerns during public comment — about how the data could be misused, and whether the cameras had actually reduced crime since they were installed in 2024 — the city council voted unanimously to end its contract with the camera company. The police chief had described safeguards the department had put in place, and said he couldn’t credit the cameras alone for the city’s drop in crime but saw them as “one piece of the wheel.” Other cities heard similar arguments and kept their cameras.

KJZZ, 2025-12-17.

Ukiah, California (December 2025). The Ukiah City Council approved the purchase of a new mobile license-plate-reader system for parking enforcement — about $53,000, paid from the city’s parking funds, replacing an older system that was wearing out. It was on the consent calendar, approved with other routine items.

Bay City News via The Mendocino Voice, 2025-12-20.

Neither decision is presented here as right or wrong. The point is how they were made. In Flagstaff, people came to the meeting and spoke. In Ukiah, the item passed as routine — which is how most local technology purchases pass. A resident who wanted to know how long the plate data would be kept, or who could see it, had a place to ask: before or at that meeting.

Try this

Find your own city or county council’s agenda page. Search its recent agendas for “camera,” “license plate,” “software” or “AI.” Is there a decision coming up that you’d want a say in?

Accurate as of 2026-10.

Two graded pieces, and getting ready for Monday.

Module 6’s studio has two graded pieces, each on its own page in this module:
