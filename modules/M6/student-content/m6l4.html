Your Portfolio, Finished

Fifteen files, and the reflection that ties them together.

Read this section

Since week 1 you’ve been filing one PDF at a time into your CSC141-portfolio folder. This week you add the last one and hand in the whole folder. It’s graded as a collection, in two ways:

  • Is everything there? Every file earns credit for being present under its own number. A file finished late still counts, under its own number. A missing file is a gap, and renumbering to hide it is the one thing that breaks the system.
  • Does it add up? The last file — your 10 Things revisit and your synthesis — is where you show what the collection means: what changed, what you can do now, and what you’ll do next.

Your module reflections are already inside your graded artifacts — Module 1’s in your bias analysis, Module 2’s in your casebook, and so on. Your Module 6 reflection is part of your synthesis. Nothing needs to be gathered separately.

Revisiting Your 10 Things

The “before” picture finally earns its keep.

Read this section

In the first session, before anything was taught, you wrote a quick list: 10 things AI can help me do in my daily life. It was ungraded on purpose, so you’d write what you actually thought. Now you know how these tools work, where they fail, what they do with your data and what they mean for work. The revisit has three parts:

  1. Revise the original list. Go item by item: keep it, change it or remove it — and add what you’d never have thought of in week 1.
  2. Write a new list: things AI can help you do in your work environment — the job you studied in Module 5, or the one you have.
  3. Annotate both. A short note on each change: what you know now that you didn’t then.

A thin annotation

Changed “write my essays” to “help with essays.” I learned more about AI.

A useful one

Changed “write my essays” to “quiz me on my notes before an exam.” In M3L2 I saw that letting a tool write for me skips the part where I learn, and my study tool in Module 3 helped more than any draft did.

Writing a Synthesis

Connect, don’t summarize.

Read this section

A summary walks through the modules in order: “In Module 1 we learned… In Module 2 we learned…” Your instructor was there; a summary tells them nothing new. A synthesis finds the threads that run across your own work and says what they add up to.

Some threads worth looking for:

  • A habit that showed up in more than one artifact — checking sources, taking names out, sorting tasks before asking a tool.
  • A belief from week 1 that your own results contradicted.
  • A moment you trusted a tool and shouldn’t have — or didn’t, and should have.
  • How your Code moved from version 1 to version 2, and what moved it.
  • What you still question. An honest open question is worth more than a tidy ending.

Point to your files by number as you go — “in my bias audit (10)” — so a reader can check what you’re claiming.

After This Course

AI literacy is a habit, not a grade.

Read this section

The tools you used this semester will be different within a year. What lasts is the set of questions you now ask: how did it produce this, is it right, who could it be unfair to, where does my data go, who’s accountable, and do I actually need it here? Your synthesis ends with a plan for keeping those questions sharp. Make it concrete:

  • One way to keep up — a news source, a professional group in your field, a newsletter. (The two AI-and-security sources from our in-class discussion are a start.)
  • One habit you’ll keep — from your Code, version 2.
  • One thing you want to learn next — using AI better in your field, building with it, or securing it.
  • When you’ll look at your Code again — your review plan, put on a calendar.
Try this

Before you write your plan, reread your 01 file. Picture the person who wrote that list in August. What would you tell them?
