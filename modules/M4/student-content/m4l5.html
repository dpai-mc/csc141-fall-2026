Your Side of the Four Questions

M4L4 asked them about systems. Now ask them about the tools you use.

Read this section

In M4L4 you asked four questions of any AI system that takes in your information: what is collected, where it’s kept, who can see it, and how long it lasts. This lesson turns them around. The tools on your phone, in your browser and in your classes each have answers to those four questions — and almost nobody looks them up.

That’s this week’s job. Your security assessment in this week’s studio asks you to find the answers for the tools you actually use, and decide what to do about them.

Count every tool, not just the chat window. AI turns up in more places than the apps with “AI” in their name:

  • The chatbots you open on purpose — Gemini for this course, and any others you use.
  • AI built into something else — a phone keyboard that suggests your next word, a search engine that writes a summary above the results, a chat assistant inside a social app, a writing helper inside an email or document editor.
  • Tools you plan to use — for a job, another class, or something on your 10 Things list.

The built-in ones are the easiest to miss, because nobody signed up for them on purpose.

Where the answers live. Look in three places, in this order:

  1. The account you’re signed in with. M1L5’s rule still decides more than anything else: the account, not the tool, sets the terms.
  2. The tool’s privacy or data settings. Look for whether your conversations are used for training, whether your history is kept, and how to delete it. Menus move between versions, so look for the idea, not a particular button.
  3. The privacy policy. You don’t need to read all of it. Search it for four things — training, review by people, how long data is kept, and deletion — the M4L4 questions, in the company’s own words.

For Gemini, Copilot, ChatGPT and Claude, the course page How the tools handle your data puts those answers side by side, with sources and dates. For anything else, you’re the researcher.

Verify

Write the date next to every answer you find. Privacy terms and settings change, sometimes quietly. “Not used for training on my account, per its settings page, October 2026” is a finding. “Not used for training” is a guess about the future.

Share Less, and Share Smarter

Most of your protection happens before you press Send.

Read this section

M4L4 ended on a hard fact: deleting a conversation acts on your history, not on a model that has already learned from it. So the protection you control most is what you put in.

M1L5 gave you two principles from the organization’s side — data minimization and anonymization. Here they are from your side of the text box.

  • Share only what the task needs. Before you paste, ask what the tool needs in order to help. To improve an email, it needs the purpose and the tone — not the recipient’s full name, your address or the account number in the signature.
  • Take out what identifies people. Replace names with roles (“my manager,” “Customer A”), round exact numbers, and drop dates and places the answer doesn’t depend on. Details that are harmless alone can identify someone together, so it’s the combination you’re breaking up.
  • Never paste someone else’s information. A classmate’s work, a coworker’s message, a customer’s details — M1L5’s rule holds everywhere. Your consent isn’t theirs to give.
  • Decide your settings once, on purpose. Where a tool lets you turn off training on your conversations, turn off history or switch off a memory feature, make that choice deliberately instead of living with the default.
  • Match the account to the task. For anything connected to school or work, use the account with an agreement behind it — for this course, your college account. Keep the most sensitive things out of every cloud tool.

Before

I work the front desk at Valley Family Clinic. A patient, Maria Delgado, born March 14, 1987, called upset that her lab results from last Tuesday were never sent to her. Write an apology email to her.

After

I work at the front desk of a medical office. A patient called upset that her test results were delayed. Write a short, warm apology email I can adapt — I’ll add the details myself.

Both versions get the same help. In the second, the tool never learns who the patient is, where she’s treated or when it happened. (Both are invented for this example.) In many jobs — a medical office among them — sending the first version would also break the workplace’s confidentiality rules.

Caution

Removing the name isn’t always removing the person. If the details you leave in would let someone who knows the situation recognize it — the only night-shift nurse on the third floor — you haven’t anonymized it. You’ve only left out a word.

Keeping It on Your Own Hardware

The privacy-first option M1L5 and M2L5 said this module would come back to.

Read this section

In M1L5 you saw a diagram of data that never leaves your device, and a promise that Module 4 would return to it as a deliberate strategy. In M2L5 you learned the vocabulary. A frontier model reaches you as a cloud-hosted service, and your data leaves your device. A local model is an open-weight model running on hardware you control. M2L5 also gave you four questions — capability, privacy, cost and control — and said privacy is the one that changes what you’re allowed to put in.

That is the whole strategy:

If the material can’t leave, bring the model to the material.

When the model runs on your own machine, your prompt has nowhere else to go. That’s why some organizations that handle confidential records run models on their own hardware on purpose.

When it makes sense

  • High-sensitivity material — health records, legal files, financial details, anything you’d never want in someone else’s logs.
  • Professional confidentiality — jobs where a contract, a law or a professional code says client information stays in-house.
  • Data sovereignty — wanting your information to stay under your control and your rules, not under terms you can’t negotiate.
  • No reliable connection — a local model still answers when the internet doesn’t.

What it costs you today

  • Capability. The largest models still live in datacenters. How big the gap is depends on the task and the month — M2L5’s warning about undated claims still applies.
  • Hardware. A machine with a lot of memory, ideally a capable graphics card, and gigabytes of disk space for each model.
  • Maintenance. A model on your machine is software you now run. Updates, security fixes and keeping the device itself safe become your job; nobody is keeping it running for you.
Caution

“Local” protects the trip, not the device. A local model keeps your prompt from crossing the network. It does nothing if the laptop it runs on is lost, shared or broken into. And an open-weight model running on someone else’s server isn’t local at all — M2L5’s caution, still true.

Note

You don’t need to install anything. Nothing in this course asks you to run a model yourself. The skill is the judgment: for one of your own uses, would a local model be worth what it costs? That question is part of your security assessment.

Three Lenses for Judging a Use

For when you have to say whether an AI use is acceptable — not just what it does.

Read this section

Both pieces of this week’s studio end in a judgment. Your bias audit asks whether a tool like the one you tested should be used to screen job applicants. Your security assessment asks whether the tools you use are worth what they cost you in privacy. Those are ethical questions, and an opinion isn’t an answer to them. A reason is.

Ethical framework (for AI). A structured set of principles used to evaluate the moral implications of AI development and use.

People have argued for centuries about which principles come first, and you don’t need to settle that here. A practical approach, used in many ethics courses, is to look at a decision through several lenses — each one a different question — and see where they agree. Three of them do most of the work for judging an AI use.

Harms and benefits

The utilitarian lens

Asks
Who is helped, who is harmed, and how badly? Does the good outweigh the harm for everyone affected — not just for the person using the tool?
In the screening
A fast screen saves a hiring manager hours. A skewed one quietly costs a qualified person a job.
In your own tools
A free tool saves you time. What you type may help train a model you’ll never control.

Rights

The rights lens

Asks
Does this respect each person’s dignity and their right to decide about their own information? Did the people affected agree, and can they find out what happened and challenge it?
In the screening
Applicants usually aren’t told a tool ranked them, and can’t ask it why.
In your own tools
The people in a group photo or a forwarded message never agreed to have it uploaded.

Fairness

The justice lens

Asks
Are people who are alike in the ways that matter treated alike? Do the costs fall on everyone, or on the same group every time?
In the screening
M4L2’s three definitions are this lens, made countable.
In your own tools
If the private option is the one you pay for, privacy goes to whoever can afford it — M4L3’s question, “who does it reach?”

Adapted from the six lenses in A Framework for Ethical Decision Making, Markkula Center for Applied Ethics, Santa Clara University (revised 2021).

The lenses can disagree. A screening tool might look good on harms and benefits — faster hiring, lower cost — and bad on rights and fairness. That disagreement isn’t a failure of the method. It’s the point where you have to decide which consideration wins here, and say why. A judgment that names its lens, and admits what the other lenses say, is one someone else can check.

A judgment has three shapes: acceptable as it is; acceptable only with conditions — which you can name; or not acceptable for this use. “Only with conditions” is often the honest answer, and the conditions are where your recommendations come from.

Module 6 comes back to frameworks at a larger scale — the rules governments and organizations write for AI. These three lenses are for judging one use at a time.

Check Yourself

Not graded, not recorded. Three questions.

Open the three questions
1. You want an AI tool’s help writing to your landlord about a broken heater. Which approach shares the least while getting the same help?
2. Which situation makes the strongest case for running a local model?
3. A company’s AI screening tool saves its recruiters many hours, but applicants are never told it was used and have no way to ask why they were turned down. Which lens most directly names the problem?
