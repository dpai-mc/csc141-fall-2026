What Algorithmic Bias Is

A system can be accurate on average and still be reliably wrong about some people.

Read this section

Module 4 is about what happens when AI gets it wrong — not by accident, once, but in the same direction, for the same people, over and over. This week is about where that comes from. Next week is about what can be done about it.

Algorithmic bias. Systematic and unfair discrepancies in AI system outputs caused by biased training data, flawed design choices, or unexamined assumptions in the development process.

The word doing the work in that definition is systematic. Every AI tool makes mistakes. Bias is when the mistakes have a shape — when they land more often on one group than another. That matters even if the system is “accurate” overall, because an average can hide a group the system almost always gets wrong. A face-analysis tool that is right 99 times in 100 for most people and wrong one time in three for a particular group is not a 97%-accurate tool to the people in that group.

Training data. The dataset used to teach a machine learning model. The quality, quantity, and representativeness of training data directly shape model behavior and potential biases.

Bias gets in through two doors: the data the system learned from, and the choices people made when they built it. The next three sections take them in turn.

Caution

“The AI was accurate” can still be true. A system can be accurate overall and biased at the same time. When someone quotes a single accuracy number, the useful question is: accurate for whom?

Bias in the Data

Two kinds you met in Module 1, and a third that’s easy to miss.

Read this section

You have already met two of the ways bias gets into data. In M1L4, sampling bias was about who got collected — a dataset that over-represents some people and under-represents others. Labeling bias was about how people tagged what was collected — the human judgments baked into the labels a system learns from. Both still apply, at much larger scale.

The third way follows from the first, and it has a name of its own in this course:

Data representation. How well a dataset reflects the diversity of the population or domain it is intended to model. Poor representation leads to biased or unreliable AI outputs for underrepresented groups.

Case — Gender Shades (2018). Researchers Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru tested three commercial face-analysis systems on the task of classifying gender from a photo. For lighter-skinned men, error rates were never worse than 0.8%. For darker-skinned women, they were 20.8%, 34.5% and 34.7%. A data set one major company used to report its own system’s accuracy was more than 77% male and more than 83% white. A system tested mostly on one kind of face will look excellent until it meets everyone else.

Historical Bias

The record existed. Getting it into the data is where it went uneven.

Read this section

You may have heard that history is written by the winners. For AI, the more useful version is narrower and easier to check: what a model learned depends on what reached its training data — and that depends on what was written down, saved, scanned and put online, by whom, and when.

Historical bias. Bias that enters because the record a system learns from is itself uneven — it reflects who was hired, written about, digitized and put online in the past, not who should have been. It can be present even when every record was collected accurately.

That last sentence is what makes historical bias different from the two you met in Module 1. Nothing was sampled badly and nothing was labeled wrong. The data can be a perfectly accurate copy of the record. The record is the problem.

Here is how a large language model’s knowledge narrows, step by step:

  1. What happened — everything people did, said and published.
  2. What was written down — newspapers, books, letters, official records.
  3. What was saved and digitized — scanned, typed in, made searchable. This costs money, so it happened first where there was money to spend.
  4. What was put on the open web — public pages a crawler can reach, not behind a library login or a paywall.
  5. What the model learned from — the text a company’s crawler collected and kept.

At every step, some of the record falls away — and it doesn’t fall away evenly. Coverage at step 5 tracks who had the institutional money and web presence at step 3 and 4, not who mattered at step 1.

Case — the New York Amsterdam News. The New York Amsterdam News, one of the country’s most important Black newspapers, has published continuously for more than a century. So the gap in how much a web-trained model “knows” about the communities it covered is not a gap in the record at step 2. It is a gap at steps 3 and 4: whose archives were digitized, indexed and made crawlable. Latimer is an AI app built around exactly that diagnosis. In December 2023 it announced a licensing deal for the paper’s archive from 1929 to the present. Latimer is built on an existing language model and pulls from its licensed sources when you ask a question — the retrieval-grounded generation you met in M3L1, with the sources chosen deliberately. The underlying model’s training is the same as before; what changes is what the app hands it to read. Whether that fixes the problem — and who gets to do it — is the question M4L3 picks up next week.

Case — Amazon’s recruiting model (reported 2018). Amazon built an experimental system to score job applicants by learning from about ten years of résumés the company had received. Most of those résumés came from men, because most of the people who had applied and been hired in tech were men. The system learned that pattern and reportedly marked down résumés containing the word “women’s,” as in “women’s chess club captain.” Amazon scrapped it. The hiring records were accurate. They were an accurate record of an uneven past.

Verify

Check what the record actually holds. When an AI tool tells you little or nothing about a person, place or event, that is a fact about what reached its data — not proof that there was nothing to find. Libraries, archives and newspaper collections often hold what the open web doesn’t.

Case details accurate as of 2026-10.

Bias in the Algorithm

Even with good data, the choices people make while building a system can tilt it.

Read this section

The second door is design: what the system was told to do, which information it was allowed to use, and what happens after it starts making decisions.

Objective-function choice

What it is: every system is built to optimize something — a number it gets better at. If that number is a stand-in for what you actually care about, the system gets very good at the stand-in.

The case: a widely used US healthcare algorithm decided which patients got extra care by predicting their future medical costs, as a stand-in for medical need. Because less money had historically been spent on Black patients with the same conditions, Black patients at a given risk score were considerably sicker than white patients at that score (Obermeyer et al., Science, 2019). Nobody told the system to use race. The choice of goal did it.

Proxy variable

What it is: see the definition below.

The case: a lending model is never shown an applicant’s race but relies heavily on home zip code. Where neighborhoods are segregated, zip code carries much of the same information, and the model can sort people by race without ever being given it.

Feedback loop

What it is: see the definition below.

The case: a 2016 study simulated a predictive-policing tool trained on past drug-arrest records. The tool sent officers to the neighborhoods with the most past arrests — which produced more arrests there, which the tool then learned from — even though survey data suggested drug use was spread far more evenly across the city.

Proxy variable. A piece of data that is not a protected characteristic itself but tracks one closely enough to stand in for it — a zip code for race, an employment gap for caregiving — so a system can treat groups unequally without ever being given the characteristic.

Feedback loop (in ML). When a system’s own outputs shape the data it later learns from, so an early skew is reinforced over time — for example, predictions that send patrols to a neighborhood where more arrests are then recorded.

Try this

Find the stand-in. Next time you see a system that ranks or scores people — a credit score, a “recommended for you” list, an app that rates drivers — ask what number it is actually optimizing, and what that number is standing in for.

From One Dataset to Millions of Decisions

What was a skew in your Module 1 dataset becomes a rule when a system applies it at scale.

Read this section

In Module 1 you looked at 50 headlines and found patterns in what was covered and what wasn’t. That was a dataset. A deployed AI system is what happens when a pattern like that becomes a rule applied to every case that passes through — every résumé, every loan file, every patient. A person with a blind spot makes a few unfair calls. A system with one makes the same call thousands of times a day, consistently, and usually without anyone looking at the individual case.

That is also why you rarely get to see the cause directly. You can’t open a model and read its training data. What you can do — and what this module trains — is look at what a system did, notice a pattern, and name the most likely source from the evidence in front of you.

Step 1 — read the job and the applicants

Open Gemini (or another tool if you prefer) in a new chat. Copy the prompt below exactly as written and send it. Don’t add to it or explain what you’re testing.

You are helping a hiring manager screen job applicants. Read the job posting and the eight applicant summaries below. Choose exactly 4 applicants to advance to a phone interview. For each of the 8 applicants, write "Advance" or "Do not advance" and one sentence explaining why. Then, in two or three sentences, explain how you decided.

Step 3 — record what it decided

A result that looks fair is a real result. Tools change, and the same prompt can come back differently each time. Check Yourself

Not graded, not recorded. Three questions.

Open the three questions
1. A model trained mostly on text from the open web can say far more about a city’s mainstream newspapers from the 1940s than about its Black-owned newspapers from the same years — even though both published every week. Which source of bias is this?
2. A lending model is never given applicants’ race, but it relies heavily on home zip code. How can it still treat racial groups unequally?
3. A hospital ranks patients for an extra-care program by predicting next year’s medical costs. Patients from a group that has historically received less care end up ranked below equally sick patients. Where did the bias enter?
