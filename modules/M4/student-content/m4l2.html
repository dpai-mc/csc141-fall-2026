From Bias to Harm

Bias is a pattern in outputs. Discrimination is what that pattern does to people.

Read this section

In M4L1 you looked at where bias comes from. This lesson is about what it does once a system is making real decisions — and about a harder question than it sounds: how would you even tell whether a system is fair?

Algorithmic discrimination. When an AI system’s outputs lead to people being treated unequally — denied an opportunity, misdiagnosed, scored differently — in a way tied to their group, whether or not anyone intended it.

You’ve seen all three kinds already. The recruiting model in M4L1 denied opportunities to applicants whose résumés looked like women’s. The healthcare algorithm under-served patients who were sicker than their scores said. And in criminal justice, risk scores are used to help decide who waits for trial in jail — unequal treatment with very high stakes. That last case is where this lesson spends most of its time, because it shows the problem at the center of fairness: people who agree that discrimination is wrong can still disagree about whether a given system is discriminating.

Three Ways to Define Fair

Each one is reasonable. Each one asks a different question.

Read this section

“Fair” sounds like one idea. When you have to measure it, it splits into several. The course uses three:

Fairness metrics. Formal criteria for judging AI outputs as fair (e.g., demographic parity, equalized odds), covered conceptually with their trade-offs — not all can be satisfied simultaneously.

Demographic parity

A fairness criterion: each group receives the positive outcome at the same rate, regardless of qualifications.

The question it asks: Did each group get through at the same rate?

What it doesn’t see: whether the people who got through were the ones who should have. If one group happened to have more qualified applicants, equal rates would mean passing over some of them.

Equalized odds

A fairness criterion: among people who truly qualify, each group receives the positive outcome at the same rate — and among people who don’t, each group is wrongly given it at the same rate.

The question it asks: Did the system make the same kinds of mistakes for each group?

What it doesn’t see: how “truly qualify” was decided. It needs a correct answer to compare against, and in real life that answer is often itself a record with a history.

Individual fairness

A fairness criterion: people who are alike in every way that matters to the decision receive the same outcome.

The question it asks: Were two people who look the same on paper treated the same?

What it doesn’t see: group patterns. A system can treat every matched pair alike and still pass one group far more often, if the groups differ in other ways — and someone has to decide what “alike in every way that matters” means.

Caution

A metric only sees the groups you measure. Every check on this page compares groups someone chose to compare. A system can pass all three by gender and fail badly by age, disability or neighborhood — and nobody will see it unless someone thinks to look.

The Case Where Both Sides Had a Point

COMPAS — one set of risk scores, two definitions of fair, two opposite conclusions.

Read this section

COMPAS is a commercial tool used by courts in several US states to score how likely a defendant is to be arrested again. In 2016, the news organization ProPublica analyzed COMPAS scores for about 7,000 people in Broward County, Florida, and followed who was actually re-arrested over the next two years.

ProPublica’s finding: among defendants who were not re-arrested, Black defendants had been labeled higher risk nearly twice as often as white defendants — about 45% compared with 23%. The mistakes fell unevenly. By the standard of equalized odds, the tool was unfair.

The company’s answer: Northpointe (now Equivant), which made COMPAS, replied that at every score, Black and white defendants were re-arrested at about the same rate. A score of 7 meant the same thing whoever it was given to. By that standard — a score means the same thing for everyone — the tool was fair.

Both analyses were largely correct. Within months, researchers working separately (Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan; and Chouldechova) showed why: when two groups’ underlying rates differ, a score cannot meet both standards at once, except in special cases. In the Broward County data, recorded re-arrest rates differed between the groups — itself a record shaped by where policing happened — so one definition had to give.

There is no neutral setting. Choosing a definition of fair is choosing whose mistakes matter most.

Verify

When someone says a system is “fair,” ask: fair by which definition? A claim of fairness without a definition can’t be checked — and a claim with one tells you which mistakes the speaker decided to accept.

Context Decides

Which definition fits depends on what the decision is and who pays for each kind of mistake.

Read this section

Since no single system can satisfy every definition, someone has to choose — and the choice is about values, not arithmetic. A useful way to make it is to ask, for this decision: which mistake does the most harm, and to whom?

  • A medical screening test. Missing a sick person can be fatal; a false alarm means another appointment. You would care most that sick people in every group are caught at the same rate — the equalized-odds question.
  • Choosing who gets a job interview. Qualified people in every group should get through at the same rate, and two applicants with the same record should be treated the same — the equalized-odds and individual-fairness questions together.
  • Spreading a limited opportunity across a community — say, who gets invited to a free summer program. Some programs decide each group should be offered places at the same rate because the goal is reach, not ranking — the demographic-parity question.

People can reasonably disagree about any of these. What isn’t reasonable is pretending no choice was made. Every system that ranks people has a definition of fair built into it, stated or not.
