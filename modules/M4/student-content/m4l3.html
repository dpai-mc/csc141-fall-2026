Four Places to Intervene

A system can be corrected before it learns, while it learns, when it answers, or after it decides.

Read this section

In M4L1, bias entered through two doors: the data a system learned from, and the choices people made building it. The fixes line up with the same path. There are four places to step in:

  1. Before training — the data (pre-processing). Collect more representative samples. Rebalance the data so an under-represented group isn’t drowned out — by adding examples or by giving them more weight. Remove a proxy variable.
  2. During training (in-processing). Tell the training process to aim at two things at once: accuracy, and one of the fairness definitions from M4L2. The system gives up a little of one to get more of the other.
  3. At question time — what the product hands the model. Add sources the original training underweighted, so the app can pull from them when a question needs them. This one leaves the model’s training exactly as it was; the next section is a real example.
  4. After the output (post-processing). Adjust the score a system needs before it approves someone. Audit outputs before a system goes live — run matched cases through it and compare, which is exactly what you did with eight applicants in M4L1.

Notice that every one of these moves a system toward one of M4L2’s definitions of fair. M4L2 also told you no system can satisfy all of them at once. So every fix is a choice about which mistakes to reduce — not a switch marked “unbiased.”

Caution

Removing a variable rarely removes the information. Take gender out of a hiring model’s data and it can still learn gender from what’s left — a gap in employment, a club, a first name. That’s M4L1’s proxy variable, coming back. A fix that only removes the obvious column can look like a solution and change very little.

The Latimer Case, Second Half

Adding the sources the crawl missed — and asking who gets to.

Read this section

In M4L1 you met Latimer: an AI app built on an existing language model that pulls from licensed archives — including the New York Amsterdam News, 1929 to the present — when you ask a question. M4L1 left two questions open. Here are the answers.

Does it fix historical bias? Partly. When the app retrieves from those archives, the model writes from a record the open-web crawl underweighted, and the answer can draw on history a web-trained model barely saw. What it doesn’t change is the model underneath. That model was trained on the same uneven crawl as before. And as you saw in M3L1, an app decides question by question whether to look something up — when it doesn’t, the model answers from that training. So this is a real reduction of historical bias for some questions, not its elimination.

Who gets to do it? The initial agreement for the archive was exclusive. The paper’s publisher said both sides were exploring how others might eventually get access, and that may happen. But the deal shows how this kind of remedy works in practice: a better record is something you license, and licenses cost money. One product can improve while every other model trained on the same uneven web stays as it was.

A remedy you have to buy reaches only the people who bought it.

That isn’t a reason to dismiss the fix. It’s a reason to ask, about any fix: who does it reach?

People in the Loop

Human review catches what a system misses — when the human actually looks.

Read this section

The most common answer to “what if the AI is wrong?” is “a person will check it.” A human in the loop reviews the system’s output before a decision takes effect. It works when the reviewer has three things: time to look at the case, information the system didn’t use, and authority to overrule it.

It fails in a predictable way. People tend to go along with a system’s recommendation — especially when they are busy, when the system is usually right, and when disagreeing takes more effort than agreeing. A reviewer approving hundreds of recommendations an hour is not reviewing; they are signing. A human in the loop is only a safeguard if the loop gives them a real chance to say no.

The Limits of Mitigation

Reduction is not elimination — and calling something fixed can be its own problem.

Read this section

Everything in this lesson reduces bias. None of it removes it. Some limits are built in: M4L2’s trade-off means a fix for one definition of fair can make another worse; removing one proxy leaves the others; a retrieval fix helps only when the app retrieves. Other limits are about honesty.

Fairness washing. Presenting an AI system as fair — through a fairness claim, a one-time audit or a mitigation step — in a way that suggests more was fixed than actually was.

Three signs: a fairness claim with no definition attached; a single audit treated as permanent, even though the system and the people using it keep changing; and a fix that is described but never measured.

Your role as a user. You can’t retrain a model. You can do what this module has been training: notice when outputs have a shape, ask fair by which definition?, test with matched cases when the stakes are high, report a biased output through the product’s feedback, and never let an AI output make a decision about a person on its own. The activity below tests the one fix you control directly — your prompt.

Verify

When someone says bias was fixed, ask three things: fixed by which definition of fair, measured how, and checked when? A claim that can’t answer all three is a claim, not a result.

Not graded, not recorded. Three questions.

Open the three questions

2. An app adds licensed historical archives that it pulls from when you ask a question. What does that change, and what does it leave alone?
