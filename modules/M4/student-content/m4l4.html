What Happens to Data in an AI System

Four questions: what’s collected, where it’s kept, who can see it, and for how long.

Read this section

The first half of this module was about AI being unfair. This lesson is about two other ways AI can go wrong for people: what happens to the information it takes in, and what it lets other people do.

In M1L5 you learned why the kind of account you use decides which protections apply to you. This lesson is about the systems behind that rule. Any AI service that takes in your information has an answer — stated or not — to four questions:

  1. What is collected? What you type and upload, and often more: voice recordings, images, the time and place you used it, and the device you used.
  2. Where is it kept? On the company’s servers, usually in more than one place — logs, backups, systems for reviewing problems.
  3. Who can see it? The company, and in some cases the people it hires to review conversations for safety or quality; partners it shares data with; authorities with a legal demand; and anyone who breaks in.
  4. How long does it last? Retention periods differ by company and by account. Deleting something from your history doesn’t always delete every copy — and, as the next section explains, it can’t undo what a model already learned.

None of these answers is visible in the chat window. They’re in policies, settings and contracts — which is exactly why a privacy problem can sit unnoticed until something goes wrong.

When Your Words Become Training Data

What a model learned from can’t easily be taken back out.

Read this section

Some services use conversations to help train future versions of their models, depending on the account and the settings (the M1L5 rule). Training doesn’t store your conversation as a file the model looks up. It adjusts the model’s parameters — millions of numbers — a little, based on everything in the training data. That has two consequences.

It’s very hard to remove. Deleting a chat removes it from your history. It does not reach into a model that was already trained on it and take back what it learned.

It can come back out. Models can sometimes reproduce text they were trained on. In a 2021 study, researchers prompted an earlier, openly available language model, GPT-2, and got it to output passages copied word for word from its training data — including some people’s names, phone numbers and email addresses (Carlini et al., 2021). Most of what a model learns is general patterns, not memorized text. But anything sensitive in training data carries a risk of resurfacing, for someone else.

Caution

“Delete” means less than it sounds like. Deleting a conversation is real and worth doing. But it acts on your history, not on a model that has already learned from it. The time a privacy choice matters most is before you share, not after.

AI and Surveillance

The pattern-matching that sorts your photos can also identify a stranger in a crowd.

Read this section

Many AI systems are, at bottom, very good at matching patterns. Pointed at people, that becomes surveillance:

  • Facial recognition — matching a face in a photo or video against a database of known faces.
  • Behavior prediction — inferring from where people go, what they buy or how they move what they are likely to do next.
  • Mass monitoring — running those systems continuously, over everyone in a space, rather than on one person under suspicion.

Case — Robert Williams (Detroit, 2020). Detroit police arrested Robert Williams at his home, in front of his family, after a facial-recognition search matched his old driver’s-license photo to a blurry image of a shoplifting suspect. He was not the man in the image. He was held for about 30 hours. In a 2024 settlement, the Detroit Police Department agreed that it will not arrest people based only on facial-recognition results.

His case shows the three civil-liberties concerns at once. Errors land on real people — and, as Gender Shades showed in M4L1, they land unevenly. Consent is missing — nobody in a database of license photos agreed to be searched for a shoplifting case. And being watched changes behavior: people who know they may be identified anywhere tend to speak, gather and protest less, which is why mass monitoring is a concern even when it works perfectly.

Try this

Count the cameras. On your next walk through a store, a campus building or a parking lot, notice how many cameras you pass. You can’t tell from looking which ones feed a recognition system. That uncertainty is part of what mass monitoring means.

AI as a Threat — and a Defense

One capability, pointed in two directions.

Read this section

The same abilities that make AI useful make it useful to attackers.

How attackers use it

  • Automated phishing. Fluent, personalized scam messages, written at scale. Spelling mistakes used to give scams away; they no longer reliably do.
  • Deepfakes for social engineering. A cloned voice on the phone, or a faked face on a video call, asking for money or access.
  • Finding weaknesses. AI that searches software for security flaws faster than people can.

How defenders use it

  • Anomaly detection. Spotting the login, transfer or network traffic that doesn’t fit the usual pattern.
  • Threat intelligence. Sorting through far more alerts and reports than a security team could read.
  • Automated response. Locking an account or isolating a machine in seconds, before a person could react.

Deepfake. AI-generated synthetic media (audio, video, images) convincingly impersonating real people.

Case — Big Sleep (2024). In late 2024, Google reported that Big Sleep, an AI agent built by its security researchers, had found an exploitable flaw in SQLite, a database engine used in an enormous amount of software. The flaw was reported and fixed before it reached an official release. A tool that can find a flaw before attackers do can, aimed differently, find it for them.

Dual-use dilemma. The same AI capabilities that enable protection and defense also enable attack and harm.

Verify

A polished message is not a trustworthy one. When a message, a call or a video asks you to act fast — send money, share a code, open a file — treat it the way you’d treat an unsourced claim from an AI tool: check it independently before you act.

M4L4-A1 — One incident, four questions

30 minutes Optional export

Have an AI tool analyze one real privacy or security incident, then check two of its claims yourself.

Step 1 — choose an incident

Pick one of these four, all real and widely reported:

  • Arup (2024) — a finance employee at the engineering firm Arup paid out about US$25 million after a video call with deepfaked senior colleagues.
  • Samsung (2023) — engineers pasted confidential source code into a public chatbot while asking it for help.
  • ChatGPT (March 2023) — a bug briefly showed some users the titles of other people’s chats.
  • Clearview AI (2024) — the Dutch data protection authority fined the company €30.5 million over its database of faces.

Or choose one you found yourself — as long as it doesn’t involve sexual imagery, isn’t about a private person, and didn’t happen to you or someone you know. Whatever you pick appears in your export.

Step 2 — ask the tool

Open Gemini (or another tool if you prefer) in a new chat. Copy the prompt, replace the bracket with your incident, and send it.

Summarize this AI-related privacy or security incident: [the incident]. Answer these four questions in plain language, two or three sentences each, and give a source with a link for each answer:
1. What data was exposed or misused?
2. Who was affected?
3. What protection failed?
4. What could the organization have done to prevent it?

Step 3 — record the analysis

Paste the whole answer, then fill the four fields in your own words — trimming, correcting or adding to what the tool said.

Step 4 — check two claims

Pick two specific claims (a number, a date, a named fact). Open the source the tool gave — or search for the claim if it gave none — and find the sentence. Use M3L1’s four kinds of wrong for the drop-down.

Step 5 — separate the AI from the rest

Which part of this incident was about AI itself, and which part was an ordinary failure that could have happened without it?

Note

Many incidents labeled “AI” turn on an ordinary failure — a missed check, a bug, a rule nobody followed. Separating the part that was about AI from the part that wasn’t is the point of Step 5.
